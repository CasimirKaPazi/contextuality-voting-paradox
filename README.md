# Impossibility, Self-Reference and Collective Choice

Work in progress on a common description of the impossibility results of social choice (Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, Young–Levenglick and others), the paradoxes of self-reference (the Liar, Gödel, the halting problem, Lawvere's fixed-point theorem) and contextuality.

The guiding observation is that all of these have the same form: local pieces that each make sense do not fit into a consistent whole, and every attempt to force a fit either produces a contradiction, imposes an arbitrary choice, or introduces chance.

**Status.** Everything here is a draft. Notes are revised as the picture becomes clearer; papers that reach a stable form will be moved to `papers/` and then left unchanged. Claims are marked throughout as cited theorems, results proved in the text, results checked by computation on small cases, or interpretation.

## Where to start

- [`notes/roadmap.md`](notes/roadmap.md) — overview of all threads, what is in hand and what is missing.
- [`notes/map-of-voting-paradoxes.md`](notes/map-of-voting-paradoxes.md) — the known voting paradoxes, grouped by what is varied, with the theorems that connect them. Includes the elementary argument that no Condorcet extension is consistent, and that the obstruction is the majority cycle itself acting on the possible ways of breaking it.
- [`notes/where-the-symmetry-goes.md`](notes/where-the-symmetry-goes.md) — aggregation into a ranking must dispose of a symmetry; it ends up in the output (a paradox), in the rule (a dictator or tie-breaker), or in a fixed point (a lottery). Includes a table sorting known results by placement.

- [`papers/drafts/`](papers/drafts/) — three short drafts: *Cycles and Twists* (the topology of a Condorcet cycle), *Walls and Strategy* (why best replies cannot be computed from inside) and *Four Levels of Obstruction in Social Choice* (local inconsistency, contextuality, symmetry breaking, consistency).

### Scripts (`scripts/`)

All scripts are short and self-contained. They need Python 3 with `numpy`; some also need `scipy` or `matplotlib`. Run them from their own directory, for example `cd scripts/consistency && python3 convexity_check.py`.

| `topology/seam_check.py` | In every Klein bottle gluing, orientation flips at every seam edge; in every torus gluing, at none | *Cycles and Twists*, Proposition 5 |
| `contextuality/condorcet_contextuality.py` | A Condorcet cycle has no valid model on a finite line, is consistent on a circle, and is strongly contextual only on an unbounded line; a choice-based encoding cannot tell cycles from orders | *Cycles and Twists* §3 |

### Formal proofs (`lean/`)

Lean 4 files that check statements from the notes and the drafts. They form one Lake project with Mathlib as dependency: run `lake build` in `lean/` (toolchain in `lean/lean-toolchain`). The older files use only core Lean; `ProbLine.lean`, `CircleTournaments.lean`, `LoopsOfRelations.lean` and `ProbabilisticArrow.lean` use Mathlib. Finite statements are proved by computation (`decide`); the others by ordinary proofs.

| File | What it proves | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| `ValueSpaces.lean` | Propositions 1–4 for cycles of any length: no model on a bounded line (assuming only that every alternative is beaten by some alternative), a placement on a circle of n points, the unbounded-line model compatible without global section, and the winding number | *Cycles and Twists* §3–4 |
| `Condorcet.lean` | The Condorcet profile has a majority cycle and no Condorcet winner; each of the 216 profiles of three voters on three alternatives has a Condorcet winner or a full cycle; exactly 12 have a cycle | background to *Cycles and Twists* §3 |
| `ProfileFacts.lean` | The bound p(a ≻ b) + p(b ≻ c) + p(c ≻ a) ≤ 2 for any profile, ties allowed, attained by the Condorcet profile; no deterministic rule on three alternatives and three voters is invariant under rotating the voters and commutes with the cyclic renaming | *Cycles and Twists* §3; *Walls and Strategy* §2 |
| `Nerve.lean` | The complex of strict orders on three alternatives is an annulus bounded by the two cycles; of the six gluings of its boundary circles, the three along the reference orientations are non-orientable and the other three orientable, and under the former every seam edge joins triangles with disagreeing orientations; filling in both cycles gives a closed orientable surface of Euler characteristic 2, on which reversing every preference is a free, orientation-reversing symmetry; identifying only the two cycle faces gives no surface | *Cycles and Twists* §5 |
| `Burial.lean` | Burial (Proposition 1 of *Walls and Strategy*) for any numbers of voters and alternatives, ballots with ties allowed | *Walls and Strategy* §2 |
| `Consistency.lean` | A consistent Condorcet extension never elects an alternative that another beats by majority (finite electorates, ties allowed, explicit counter-electorate); hence none exists at a profile where every alternative is beaten | *Consistency as an Extension Problem*; *Map of Voting Paradoxes* |
| `Diagonal.lean` | Lemma 2 in abstract form: without a dominant ballot, no strategist is universally correct against opponents that can run it. The only computational assumption is that the opponents can apply any map to the strategist's output | *Walls and Strategy* §3 |
| `Arrow.lean` | Arrow's theorem in the form used by Gibbard (1973): orderings with ties, unanimity, pairwise determination, at least three outcomes | input to `Gibbard73.lean` |
| `Gibbard73.lean` | Gibbard (1973): every straightforward game form with at least three possible outcomes is dictatorial, following Gibbard's Section 3 and his definitions | *Walls and Strategy* §3 |
| `Dichotomy.lean` | Theorem 3: every game form with at least three outcomes is dictatorial or has a player and an ordering for which no strategist is universally correct | *Walls and Strategy* §3 |
| `Lotteries.lean` | Theorem 6 (lotteries as outcomes: regress or ex ante inefficiency) from Lemma 2, with Gibbard's 1978 Corollary 2 entering as an explicit hypothesis | *Walls and Strategy* §4 |
| `RationalDiagonal.lean` | Proposition 4 for plurality, Borda, instant runoff and Black's rule with two and three voters, by checking certificates found by search; the count 12 of 36 in the veto-then-pick example | *Walls and Strategy* §3 |
| `NonTight.lean` | Every two-player game form that is not tight admits a rational diagonal with strict preferences: player 1 ranks a set S it cannot force on top, player 2 has the reverse order | *Walls and Strategy*, Proposition 5 |
| `ProbLine.lean` | Remark (d): if every alternative of a finite set is beaten by one in the set, no family of probability distributions on the real line realises this, even with only single-alternative marginals agreeing | *Cycles and Twists* §3 |
| `CircleTournaments.lean` | A tournament placed on the circle is locally transitive; with four alternatives, one beating every member of a cycle has no placement on the circle but a compatible probabilistic model (uniform on seven points) | *Cycles and Twists* §3 |
| `LoopsOfRelations.lean` | Propositions 1–3 of *Loops of Relations*: sections are fixed points of the monodromy; on finite value sets a compatible loop closes on a finite cover; with a free group action a section exists exactly when the holonomy set contains the identity, and the loop run k times has one exactly when the order of the holonomy divides k; an adversarial edge breaks every fixed point exactly when there is no universal fixed point | *Loops of Relations* |
| `ProbabilisticArrow.lean` | Theorem 5: for three alternatives and any finite set of voters, every empirical model on Arrow's scenario is a mixture of dictatorships | *Four Levels* §5 |
| `PathPinning.lean` | The pinning step of Theorem 5′: at a completed profile, the probability of a ranking is the total weight of the voters ranking it, given the marginals on the alternatives other than its second and other than its last | *Four Levels* Theorem 5′ |
| `ArrowGeneral.lean` | Theorem 5′ from the three-alternative case: a general framework (rankings as strict total orders on any finite type, empirical models with full compatibility for any set of pairs and unanimity, mixtures of dictatorships), the restriction of a model to fewer alternatives, and the induction on the number of alternatives with the completion constructed explicitly | *Four Levels* Theorem 5′ |
| `ArrowBase.lean` | The passage between the general framework and `ProbabilisticArrow.lean` for three alternatives, and Theorem 5′ (`theorem5'`): for any number ≥ 3 of alternatives and any finite set of voters, every empirical model on Arrow's scenario is a mixture of dictatorships | *Four Levels* Theorem 5′ |

What the files leave to standard topology: that a coherent orientation of the triangles is the same as orientability, and the classification of surfaces, which names the resulting surfaces (torus, Klein bottle, sphere, projective plane). What is not formalised: the count over all 18,138 two-player game forms, whose formal version would also need a proof that the enumeration is complete, and Gibbard's 1978 Corollary 2, which `Lotteries.lean` assumes.

## Related work

The notes build on, among others: Livson, Prokopenko and Pritam on Arrow and Gödel and on the non-orientable topology of Condorcet's paradox; Abramsky and co-authors on contextuality and on Arrow's theorem in categorical form; Gibbard (1973, 1977, 1978); Young and Levenglick; Moulin; Saari's geometry of voting; Jiang, Lim, Yao and Ye on combinatorial Hodge theory and ranking; McKelvey on intransitivity in spatial voting; Binmore, Anderlini, and Nachbar and Zame on non-computable strategies, and Canning on which domains of games and opponents allow computable rational play; Baryshnikov and Root, and Rajsbaum and Raventós-Pujol, on topological proofs of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite and Arrow theorems. Full references are given in each document. References marked † in the notes were cited from memory and have not yet been checked against the originals.
