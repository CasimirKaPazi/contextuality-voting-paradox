# Roadmap: Impossibility, Self-Reference and Collective Choice

*Status overview of the work in progress, November 2026. It lists the threads that have emerged, the central claim of each, what is already in hand, and what a rigorous treatment would still require. All documents named here are drafts.*

## The overall picture

The impossibility theorems of social choice share a common form with the Liar paradox, incompleteness and the halting problem: local pieces that each make sense fail to fit into a consistent whole, and every attempt to force a fit either produces a contradiction, imposes an arbitrary choice, or introduces chance. In current form (*Contextuality and Symmetry*, Section 3a, and the draft *Four Levels of Obstruction in Social Choice*), the obstructions sort into four levels:
- local inconsistency: no locally consistent model at all, for instance Young–Levenglick read for joint draws of winners;
- contextuality: locally consistent models without a global one;
- symmetry breaking: global solutions exist but none is symmetric, as in Arrow's theorem;
- consistency.

These are the levels of sheaf-theoretic contextuality with symmetry breaking added, and they match the bounded line, unbounded line and circle of *Cycles and Twists*, with one qualification: the unbounded line is contextual only possibilistically, and the probabilistic second level appears on the circle for tournaments that are not locally transitive.

The threads below develop different parts of this picture. Two of them carry the general description; the others are applications, special cases or speculative extensions.

## Core threads

### A. Consistency as non-contextuality (the heart)

**Claim.** Aggregation is a local-to-global problem in the sense of sheaf-theoretic contextuality, with sub-electorates (and, separately, subsets of alternatives) as contexts. The consistency criterion of voting is a gluing condition; demanding it at the level of winners amounts to assuming non-contextuality. Young–Levenglick, Moulin's no-show theorem and Arrow's theorem are then obstructions to gluing of different kinds, and Kemeny's rule is the case in which gluing holds for full sections (rankings) but fails after projection to a winner.

**In hand.** An elementary proof, for vote shares and any number of candidates, that every Condorcet extension fails consistency at every profile without a Condorcet winner: electing any candidate is undone by mixing with a profile in which a candidate that beats it becomes the Condorcet winner. Consistency is convexity of winner regions. The map of paradoxes and its patterns; the Kemeny analysis; the plurality example in which a consistent rule elects a candidate on a profile where every pairwise comparison is tied; the observation that Condorcet cycles become contextual only on an unbounded value space, and there only possibilistically; the Hodge and Saari decompositions as the real-valued structure of pairwise data.

**In hand (joining A and B).** Aggregation written as a constraint network: variables are the social decisions on each pair for each column of voters' bits, contexts are profiles, independence of irrelevant alternatives is non-contextuality of these decisions. Integral solutions are the dictators; symmetric ones do not exist; the symmetric fractional solution is random dictatorship. Agreement-type obstructions (no solution at all: Young–Levenglick, no-show) are distinguished from symmetry-breaking ones (solutions exist, none symmetric: Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite), as in distributed computing; by Kun et al., the symmetry can be kept integrally exactly when the local-consistency relaxation is exact. Empirical models on this scenario, in the sense of Abramsky and Brandenburger, are exactly the mixtures of dictatorships (proved for three alternatives and any number of voters, *Four Levels*, Theorem 5, checked in Lean; for any number of alternatives and voters, Theorem 5′, by a short induction, checked in Lean): the scenario is non-contextual, with the dictator as hidden variable, and becomes strongly contextual when divided by the voters' permutations. Random rules satisfying only Barberà and Sonnenschein's pairwise independence can go beyond the mixtures from three voters on (the coin rule), but only by signalling across pairs. See *Contextuality and Symmetry*.

**In hand (electorates).** Young–Levenglick written as a scenario with electorates in the contexts: read as a joint draw of winners, no rule that elects every Condorcet winner, deterministic or lottery-valued, is consistent; the obstruction is a failure of local consistency, not contextuality. Maximal lotteries are consistent only ex ante. The no-show theorem splits in the same way: SD-participation, which is participation for joint draws, fails for every Condorcet rule (Brandt, Geist and Peters), while maximal lotteries satisfy participation for independent draws (Brandl, Brandt and Hofbauer). See *Consistency as an Extension Problem*, Section 3b; both constraints in one scenario, with joins and one-voter extensions as contexts, in Section 3c (for three alternatives no deterministic rule from 8 voters and no lottery rule drawn jointly from 9; participation adds nothing there).

**In hand (the certificates).** At the first level the obstruction is a dual certificate of the linear program, not a cohomology class, and for three alternatives it can be read off at both thresholds (*Consistency as an Extension Problem*, Section 3d). For lotteries drawn jointly (nine voters) the least certificate is three joins around one profile, the doubled Condorcet profile: for each candidate x, a three-voter electorate with Condorcet winner x whose join with the profile elects the candidate that beats x. That is the blocking lemma at a single profile, with small witnesses, and the same shape holds at ten and eleven voters. For deterministic rules (eight voters) no profile is blocked on its own. A minimal unsatisfiable set has ten profiles, and the proof is a case split at the Condorcet profile in which the blocking witness is derived by consistency on two tied four-voter profiles. Determinism lets a tie be resolved once and reused as a witness, and that is the one voter between the thresholds. Neither certificate uses covers by more than two parts or overlapping parts. At eight voters there are empirical models, with draws agreeing on shared pairs of profiles, but no global section. Relative to the renamings, the scenario therefore passes through all four levels as the electorate grows: level 4 up to two voters, level 3 from three to seven (isotropy at the Condorcet profile), level 2 at eight, level 1 from nine.

**Still required.** A sheaf formulation with sub-electorates as the cover that goes beyond the scenario of triples; the relation between the electorate-indexed and the alternative-indexed versions (consistency versus IIA), of which *Contextuality and Symmetry* gives a first reading (locality in the direction of alternatives, gluing in the direction of voters).

**Documents.** *Consistency as an Extension Problem* (the current formulation: consistency as convexity in vote-share space, the Condorcet winner as a partial assignment whose consistent extension across the cycle region is impossible, and a table of which combinations of symmetry, gluing and cycle sensitivity are possible, with maximal lotteries as the unique rule achieving all three); *A Map of Voting Paradoxes*; *Two Kinds of Loop*; Sections 3–4 of *Cycles and Twists*.

### B. Where the symmetry goes (the question that follows)

**Claim.** Aggregation into a rigid total order must dispose of a fixed-point-free symmetry of the profile. The symmetry ends up in the output (a paradox), in the rule (a dictator, tie-breaker or agenda), or in a fixed point that is not a single alternative (a lottery). Incompatible combinations of criteria are different decisions about where it goes.

**In hand.** The elementary observation at the Condorcet profile; the three placements and their mathematical backing (rigidity of finite orders, Lawvere, Brouwer, Kakutani, Scott); a classification of known theorems by placement (see the table in *Where the Symmetry Goes*); the recursion for voters who are themselves composed. The pointwise form of the trichotomy: by the isotropy lemma, a neutral rule must send each profile to an output fixed by its stabiliser, and the three placements are the cases "an admissible fixed output exists", "only an inadmissible one exists", "none exists". This recovers, by computation on small cases, the known existence conditions for anonymous and neutral rules (Moulin; Bubboloni and Gori). The Equality Criterion of Wolk, Quinn and Ogren asks a rule to ignore the reversal-symmetric part of a profile (a ballot with its reverse): rules on pairwise margins pass, plurality and random ballot fail, and with Theorems 5 and 5′ or with Gibbard (1977) it excludes every unanimous fully independent lottery and every strategy-proof Pareto-optimal one (working note *The Equality Criterion and Reversal*).

**In hand (the global form).** Arrow's theorem as a degree (working note *The Global Form*): each voter's degree is the winding of the social ranking while that voter goes once around the hexagon of rankings. Unanimity makes the degrees sum to one; independence of irrelevant alternatives makes them −1, 0 or 1 and turns a non-zero degree into a dictatorship or anti-dictatorship. This is a short form of Baryshnikov's homological proof. For lotteries the degree is an expected winding, fixed by the lotteries along the loop; for the empirical models it equals the weights of Theorems 5 and 5′, so integer degrees with anonymity have no solution and real ones have exactly random dictatorship. The coin rule, with only pairwise independence, has a degree that depends on the context. The pivotal path is half of the diagonal loop, closed in the quotient by reversal, and a voter's Condorcet loop is twice it. Gibbard–Satterthwaite takes the same form, with outcomes on a triangle: strategy-proofness lets the outcome follow a voter only forward, so degrees are 0 or 1 and a degree 1 is a dictator; for SD-strategy-proof lotteries the real degree is always a class, unanimity gives Gibbard's weights, and without unanimity the degrees measure contextuality: for three alternatives and any number of voters the strategy-proof lotteries are the mixtures of deterministic strategy-proof rules and the voters' random top twos (degree −½), and the contextual fraction is twice the sum of the negative degrees (from Gibbard 1977). For one voter the degree is the top layer of a stack of degrees, one per dimension of face, that gives coordinates for the rule (a first look). For three alternatives, of anonymity, neutrality and reversal symmetry only anonymity constrains the degrees, and the three placements are the three ways out of n · d = 1: leave the diagonal (a dictator), leave the integers (a lottery) or leave the circle (cycles or ties in the output); the same holds for more alternatives. In the electorate direction Young–Levenglick carries no degree: the Condorcet regions wind once around the cycle region, but every point of it is blocked separately, so the obstruction is pointwise, as its level in *Four Levels* says.

**In hand (the stages).** For three alternatives, the symmetry problem as an extension problem for equivariant maps from the profile complex to the hexagon, taken apart in the order of equivariant obstruction theory (working note *Equivariant Obstructions*). Stage 0 is the isotropy lemma (the arithmetic conditions in n). Stage 1, at the midpoints of steps, never obstructs but forces pivots at even splits, May's theorem at a tie. Stage 2 is the degree: for a rule symmetric under a group of voters P, the obstruction is the class of 1 in ℤ/g, g the gcd of the orbit sizes, a torsion class that vanishes over the reals; it needs only locality and Pareto, not IIA, and it is sharp for continuous maps. Two layers outside homotopy theory follow. Positivity: Pareto pins every degree to 0 or 1, so a symmetric local Pareto rule exists exactly when P fixes a voter, which is stronger than the torsion class (blocks of two and three voters); for lotteries the degrees lie in [0, 1], which gives the direct proof, without Gibbard (1977), that unanimous SD-strategy-proof lotteries have non-negative degrees. Rigidity: IIA turns degree 1 into a dictator; for three voters there are local Pareto rules that are not dictatorships. A dictator is a vertex of the simplex of degree vectors fixed by the symmetry, random dictatorship its barycentre.

For four alternatives a transposition fixes a circle of tied ballots, and stage 2 can obstruct on its stratum where the top stage does not: no continuous neutral map is unanimous and symmetric within blocks of two and three voters, because on the stratum of a swap of the two combined with a transposition the other three voters face a three-alternative problem with ℤ/3. In general each stratum obstructs by ℤ/g_h, with g_h the gcd of the sizes of the classes of short cycles. Signs cancel as at the top. As a consequence, from four alternatives on, a continuous unanimous map that is neutral and symmetric within blocks exists only when some voter is alone in a block: neutrality plays the part that Pareto plays for three alternatives.

**Still required.** The lower stages for discrete (local) rules with four or more alternatives, and for groups of voters that are not products of symmetric groups; an equivariant form of positivity; the no-show theorem in the same light, though, like Young–Levenglick, it involves no group and probably belongs to thread A.

**Documents.** *Where the Symmetry Goes*; *Contextuality and Symmetry*; *The Global Form*; *Equivariant Obstructions*; Section 4 of *Walls and Strategy*; the draft *Four Levels of Obstruction in Social Choice*.

## Further threads

### C. Cycles and twists

The integer winding obstruction of Condorcet cycles versus the parity obstruction of the Liar; the location of non-orientability at the seam of the Klein bottle; the realised case as the sphere divided by preference reversal. Propositions 1–5, remark (d) and the easy direction of the circle characterisation are checked in Lean, as are Propositions 1–3 of *Loops of Relations*. For more than three alternatives, Proposition 6 builds the space on all comparisons at once: the realised complex is a sphere of dimension n(n − 1)/2 − 1 with reversal antipodal, the complex of strict orders is a homotopy sphere of dimension n − 2 (the sphere of rankings, the gradient part of the pairwise data), and the parity persists for every n while orientability holds for four and five alternatives; non-orientability is the parity counted once per comparison. On the pivotal-voter path from a unanimous ranking to its reverse, the twist sits at the pivotal voter: a dictatorship makes the whole half turn there, while a mixture of dictatorships crosses the fixed point of reversal at the weighted median of the switching order (§5). Still required: a counterpart of the Klein bottle gluing for n ≥ 4 (open problem 1), one statement covering both loops and the diagonal of thread D (a loop of contexts with an edge map per context, and three cases: no fixed point, fixed points of the square only, a fixed point), review by a reader from topology or social choice. A first form of the common statement is in *Loops of Relations*: sections of a loop are fixed points of its monodromy; with group-valued edges the Liar has holonomy of order two and the Condorcet cycle of infinite order, related by reduction mod 2; on finite value sets every contextual loop closes on a finite cover; the strategic diagonal is the case of an edge chosen by an adversary, where the obstruction is the absence of universal fixed points (dominant or strict Nash strategies), not of existential ones. *Documents: Cycles and Twists; The Switch Between Fixed Point and No Fixed Point (literature for the common statement: acyclicity, odd and even loops through negation, alternating fixpoints, partiality); Loops of Relations.*

### D. Strategy as a halting problem

Walls in profile space; the dichotomy between dictatorship and non-computable best replies (Gibbard 1973 with a diagonal lemma, Theorem 3, checked in Lean from Gibbard's definitions), its lottery version (Gibbard 1978, Theorem 6, in Lean with Gibbard's Corollary 2 assumed), the rational-opponent diagonal (verified on small cases, with Lean certificates for four rules), and the boundary set by sequential moves with visible votes. The diagonal lemma is in substance Canning (1992), Theorem 3, and the rational diagonal is the absence of a strict Nash strategy in the sense of his Theorem 5. For two players, every form that is not tight admits a rational diagonal (Proposition 5, in Lean), and so does every totally tight form (Proposition 5′: by hand for three outcomes, by a complete search over finite summaries for four, and for any number of outcomes by a reduction to at most seven and an exact search; for forms with a core, one step of the reduction is checked on all cores enumerated so far; working note *The rational diagonal on totally tight game forms*). Still required: the remaining tight forms (among them the normal forms of general games of perfect information and veto procedures), a hand proof for the totally tight ones beyond three outcomes, the case of randomised strategists, and the case of sequential voting in random order with private preferences. *Document: Walls and Strategy.*

### E. A ladder of lost properties

The normed division algebras and the Hopf fibrations as a frame for successive breakdowns: ordering (cycles), commutativity (agenda dependence), associativity (grouping dependence); triality and the symmetric group on three alternatives. Speculative, except for one elementary fact of independent use: proportional lotteries compose consistently across any grouping, which no deterministic majority hierarchy does. *Document: A Ladder of Lost Properties.*

### F. Applied: rules weighted by measured inconsistency

A voting rule combining a gradient signal (score differences) with pairwise margins, weighted by the measured local inconsistency of the pairwise data (curl on triangles). Tested in the Ballot Front Finder Lab, with a negative result. Weighting by Hodge curl loses Condorcet winners even without any majority cycle, because curl is cardinal and the Condorcet property ordinal: transitive triangles circulate too (honest Condorcet efficiency 88.8 against 91.3 for Smooth-MM-D; under full strategy WME\* 90.9). Re-deciding only the part of a margin that a majority cycle contests (the smaller of the margin and the strongest beatpath back) is Condorcet-consistent, but then it can differ from Smith//Score only in elections without a Condorcet winner, and it ties with it. Under strategy the lever is exaggeration: a per-voter clip on score differences helps, and widening the clip lowers WME\* steadily without raising VSE\*. What resists strategy is bounded influence per voter, not an acyclic second signal. Under full strategy all good methods sit just below "Condorcet winner, else utility winner"; finer distinctions wait on the lab's strategy model.

### G. Positioning consensus with a random fallback

Theorem 6 of *Walls and Strategy* gives a formal frame: the random fallback avoids the strategic regress at the cost of ex ante efficiency; deliberation recovers efficiency and carries the regress. This positions the CORE mechanism relative to Gibbard's theorems and to the proportional consensus of Heitzig, Simmons and Constantino. First pass in *CORE and the Formal Results*: CORE's fallback, uniform random ballot, is characterised from two directions (strategy-proofness by Gibbard and Hylland; full independence with unanimity and anonymity by *Four Levels*, Theorems 5 and 5′) and examined from a third (it is consistent and participatory for joint draws, which no Condorcet-respecting fallback is, but so is every scoring rule); the draw is the hidden variable of random dictatorship, and the paper's fair-call results are a martingale statement about it; Theorems 3 and 6 of *Walls and Strategy* make the paper's analogy with incompleteness exact for every formalisation of deliberation as a finite game form, not for open deliberation itself. The CORE paper stays a standalone introduction to the method; the new results are collected for a follow-up. *Documents: CORE and the Formal Results; Outline of a Follow-up to the CORE Paper; papers/CORE_13.md.*

## Suggested order

1. Formal checks of the newer results: done (`ProbabilisticArrow.lean`, `LoopsOfRelations.lean`, `ProbLine.lean`, `CircleTournaments.lean`).
2. Theorem 5 beyond three alternatives: done for any number of alternatives and voters (*Four Levels*, Theorem 5′; working note *Theorem 5 for any number of alternatives*). The probability of a ranking depends only on the columns of its consecutive pairs; after completing the profile so that a₂ is never above both or below both of a₁ and a₃ with a₁ above a₃, it equals the probability of the ranking with a₂ removed, which the model on one alternative fewer fixes. The whole proof is checked in Lean (`PathPinning.lean`, `ArrowGeneral.lean`, `ArrowBase.lean`); the earlier computations (m ≤ 10 along paths, m ≤ 6 for all shapes) serve as independent checks.
3. Gibbard–Satterthwaite placed (working note *Gibbard–Satterthwaite as a Scenario*): with contexts the pairs of profiles differing in one ballot, joint draws are, for whole rules, equivalent to SD-strategy-proofness, and with unanimity the empirical models are exactly the mixtures of dictatorships (from Gibbard 1977), so the scenario sits with Arrow's at level 3. Weak and full compatibility coincide there. Without unanimity there are contextual models already for one voter.
4. C: content complete for now, with Proposition 6 (more alternatives) added; the repository is the publication. D: the rational diagonal holds for every totally tight form (Proposition 5′): by hand for three outcomes, by complete search for four, and for any number by a reduction to at most seven outcomes and an exact search (working note *The rational diagonal on totally tight game forms*). Tight forms that are not totally tight remain open.
5. Thread A: one scenario with electorates as contexts, with consistency on joins and participation on one-voter extensions: done (*Consistency as an Extension Problem*, Section 3c). For three alternatives it fails from 8 voters (deterministic) and 9 (lotteries drawn jointly), exactly as consistency alone; participation adds nothing there. The certificates at both thresholds are read off in Section 3d: the blocking lemma at the Condorcet profile, direct for lotteries and with one derived witness for deterministic rules. At eight voters the draws can be made to agree on shared pairs, so the scenario is at level 2 there, between level 3 below and level 1 above. Open: four or more alternatives, and covers by more than two overlapping sub-electorates, the step towards the sheaf formulation.
6. G (the bridge to CORE): the results are collected in *CORE and the Formal Results* (Sections 2 to 3i). They go into a follow-up to the CORE paper, not into the paper itself. A proposed structure, with what is settled and what is still missing, is in *Outline of a Follow-up to the CORE Paper*. In hand:
   - the fallback, singled out from strategy and from aggregation, but not by electorate conditions alone;
   - the draw as a martingale, and holdings as the endowment plus the surpluses of the groups;
   - the floor against rational groups, with no harm possible on a line with losses proportional to distance, and attitudes to risk deciding who can agree;
   - the mean of the ideals on a line, the mean rule beyond it, and the safe agreements as the poolings of the call;
   - the call as a game solved by backward induction, one Raiffa step per group;
   - private attitudes costing nothing and private ideals bringing the barrier back;
   - proposals inside the call, with the trade-off between protecting excluded members and robustness against a stubborn member.

   Next: the steps listed before drafting in the outline, then the open questions (a closed form of the floor beyond the line, private ideals in the call game, credible stated positions, sizes of proposer groups). Then E; F is settled for now (see above).
