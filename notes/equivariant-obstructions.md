# Equivariant Obstructions: the Stages of the Symmetry Problem

*Working note for thread B of the roadmap. It writes the symmetry problem of aggregation, for three alternatives, as an extension problem for equivariant maps and takes it apart in the order of equivariant obstruction theory. The isotropy lemma of* Where the Symmetry Goes *is the obstruction at points. Ties give a condition at the midpoints of steps. The degree of* The Global Form *is the last obstruction, and it is a torsion class of order n. Two layers follow that are not homotopy-theoretic: the Pareto condition pins every degree to 0 or 1, and independence of irrelevant alternatives turns degree 1 into a dictator. The three placements then become statements about this ladder: a dictator is a vertex of the simplex of degree vectors fixed by the symmetry, a lottery is its barycentre, and a paradox is a target space whose loops contract. Proved statements, computed checks and interpretation are marked. Checks:* `scripts/symmetry/equivariant_stages.py`, `scripts/symmetry/strata_obstructions.py`.

## 1. Setting

Take three alternatives. As in *The Global Form*, the six rankings form a hexagon Y, two rankings being adjacent when they differ in one comparison. Profiles of n voters form the product X = Yⁿ, a complex of cubes: a vertex is a profile, an edge is a step of one voter, a square is a step of each of two voters, and so on. The diagonal Δ ⊂ X consists of the unanimous profiles and is a copy of Y.

**Symmetries.** The group G = Sₙ × D₆ acts on X:
- the voters' permutations Sₙ permute the factors and act trivially on Y;
- the dihedral group D₆, of order 12, consists of the renamings of the alternatives (S₃) and reversal. It acts on every factor and on Y.

On the hexagon the renamings act as follows. A 3-cycle is a rotation by two steps. A transposition is a reflection through the midpoints of two opposite edges. Reversal is the half rotation, and reversal composed with a transposition is a reflection through two opposite vertices. Only the last kind fixes a ranking.

**Rules.** A rule F sends profiles to rankings. Two conditions take the place of independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) for most of the note.
- F is **local** if a step of one voter moves the social ranking by at most one step on the hexagon.
- F is **Pareto** if it respects every comparison on which all voters agree.

Every IIA rule is local (*The Global Form*, Section 1), and every unanimous IIA rule into rankings is Pareto. Lemma 1 of *The Global Form* uses only locality: around a square the social ranking takes at most four steps, which cannot go around the hexagon. So a local rule has a degree deg_i for every voter, independent of the context, and the vector d = (deg₁, …, degₙ) is the class of F in H¹(X; ℤ) = ℤⁿ. Lemma 2 there uses IIA only on the staircase, where every voter agrees on the two comparisons that the joint step leaves alone. Pareto keeps the social ranking on the edge between the two diagonal rankings, which is all the lemma needs. So for a local Pareto rule the degrees sum to 1. *(Proved.)*

A symmetry condition is a subgroup K of G, and F is K-equivariant if F(k·x) = k·F(x). Anonymity is K = Sₙ, neutrality K = S₃, reversal symmetry the subgroup of order 2 generated by reversal, and any combination or any subgroup in between is allowed. Write P for the image of K in Sₙ. For example, a rule may be symmetric only within blocks of voters, or only under swapping two voters together with reversing all ballots.

**Subdivision.** Equivariant obstruction theory asks that a cell mapped to itself by a group element be fixed pointwise. A transposition of alternatives can swap the two ends of a step, and a permutation of voters can reflect a square, so the hexagon and X are subdivided barycentrically. The midpoint of a step from r to r′ is the ballot that is indifferent on the pair the step flips, a weak order with one tie. So the new vertices of X are the profiles in which some voters are indifferent on one pair, and the midpoints of Y are the six weak orders with one tie.

## 2. The stages

For an equivariant map into Y, the extension over a cell whose stabiliser is H meets an obstruction in π_{k−1}(Y^H) at dimension k, where Y^H is the part of Y that H fixes (Bredon; tom Dieck, Section II.3†). For the subdivided hexagon, Y^H is:
- all of Y, a circle, if H acts trivially on Y, that is, if H only permutes voters;
- two opposite rankings, for a vertex reflection;
- two opposite midpoints, for an edge reflection;
- empty otherwise.

So the stages are 0, 1 and 2. Since π_k of a circle vanishes for k ≥ 2, nothing beyond stage 2 can obstruct.

### Stage 0: points

At a profile x with stabiliser H, F(x) must lie in Y^H. This is the isotropy lemma of *Where the Symmetry Goes*, Section 7. Without anonymity it never obstructs, because a stabiliser that permutes no voters fixes every ballot, so it fixes some ranking. With anonymity:

| Symmetry | Stage 0 fails for | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| anonymity and neutrality | gcd(n, 6) > 1 | for 2 dividing n, a profile of pairs r, τr for a transposition τ; for 3, the Condorcet profile |
| anonymity and reversal | n even | a profile of pairs r, reverse of r |
| anonymity, neutrality and reversal | gcd(n, 6) > 1 | as the first row |
| single winners, anonymity and neutrality | 3 dividing n | Moulin's condition for three alternatives |

*(Proved for "fails"; computed for "only then", n ≤ 10.)* These are the arithmetic conditions recovered in *Where the Symmetry Goes*, Section 7. They depend on n through its prime factors up to the number of alternatives.

### Stage 1: midpoints

Let a step of one voter go from the profile x, with ballot r, to x′, with ballot r′, and let an element of K swap its two ends. On the hexagon it swaps r and r′, so it is the transposition τ of the pair the step flips, and it must also fix the multiset of the other ballots (with anonymity; without it, every other ballot, which is possible only for one voter). Then F(x′) = τF(x). Since τ fixes no ranking, F(x′) ≠ F(x), and by locality the two are adjacent, on an edge that τ maps to itself. Such an edge flips the same pair: the moving voter is **pivotal** there. On the subdivision this is stage 0 at the midpoint x̄, the profile in which the voter is indifferent on the pair: F(x̄) must be a midpoint fixed by τ. *(Proved.)*

This is May's theorem at a tie. The other voters are split evenly on the pair, as τ requires, and a neutral rule must let the last voter decide. It does not obstruct: dictatorships satisfy it, and Y^H is never empty at a midpoint. But it forces pivots:
- with anonymity and neutrality, at the steps where the other ballots come in pairs r, τr. There are none for n even and some for n odd, for example 18 of the 126 steps for three voters;
- with neutrality and one voter, at every step. The outcome then moves at every step. Two rankings two steps apart are carried into each other by a 3-cycle, which fixes no ranking, so their outcomes differ and the outcome never steps back. Its degree is ±1. In fact only the dictatorship and the anti-dictatorship are local, both of degree 1. *(Proved; the last statement computed.)*

### Stage 2: the degree as a torsion class

Unanimity prescribes F on Δ: the identity of Y. The question is whether it extends to an equivariant map on X, and the last obstruction is the degree.

**Proposition 1.** Let F be a local, Pareto and K-equivariant rule, and let g be the greatest common divisor of the sizes of the orbits of P on the voters. Then the degree vector is constant on the orbits of P and sums to 1. Integer vectors with both properties exist exactly when g = 1, so the rule can exist only then. The obstruction is the class of 1 in ℤ/g. *(Proved.)*

*Proof.*
- **Constant on orbits.** Let k = (σ, γ) be in K. The map k acts on H¹(X) by permuting the coordinates by σ and multiplying them by the degree χ(γ) = ±1 of γ on the hexagon, and γ acts on H¹(Y) by the same χ(γ). Equivariance gives k*[F] = F*γ*[Y], so χ(γ)·σd = χ(γ)·d, and σd = d.
- **The sum.** The restriction H¹(X) → H¹(Δ) is d ↦ Σ deg_i, and unanimity requires 1. On vectors constant on the orbits O, the sum is Σ_O |O|·d_O, whose values form gℤ. ∎

The renamings and reversal drop out of the proof because they act with the same sign on both sides, as in *The Global Form*, Proposition 6. Only P matters. It is the image of K and not its intersection with Sₙ, so a symmetry that swaps two voters and reverses all ballots at the same time counts as swapping the voters.

**In the language of obstruction theory.** For K = P, an equivariant map is a map from X/P to Y. Here X/P is a product of symmetric products of the hexagon, one per block, and each symmetric product of a circle is homotopy equivalent to a circle. Morton shows that the n-th symmetric product is a bundle of (n − 1)-simplices over a circle, orientable exactly for n odd. His proof also shows the arithmetic directly: one voter going once around moves the base by 1/n of a turn, so the diagonal loop is n times the generator. So H¹(X/P) = ℤ^b for b blocks, and its restriction to Δ is (d_j) ↦ Σ_j |block j|·d_j. The exact sequence of the pair (X/P, Δ) gives H²(X/P, Δ; ℤ) ≅ ℤ/g ⊕ H²(X/P), with H²(X/P) free. The primary obstruction to extending the unanimous map is the image of 1 under the connecting map. It is the generator of the torsion part ℤ/g. *(Proved.)*

**Torsion and lotteries.** With real coefficients ℤ/g ⊗ ℝ = 0, and the obstruction vanishes. With rational coefficients it vanishes as well, and ℤ[1/g] suffices. For full anonymity the solution is d_i = 1/n, random dictatorship, whose probabilities are multiples of 1/n, exactly the denominators the torsion requires. Placement (iii) is possible because the global obstruction is torsion. Convexification kills torsion. *(The first statements proved; the reading of placement (iii) is interpretation.)*

**For continuous maps without Pareto the stage is sharp.** On circles, the map (x₁, …, xₙ) ↦ x₁^{a₁} ⋯ xₙ^{aₙ}, in complex notation, is unanimous exactly when Σ aᵢ = 1. If the aᵢ are constant on the orbits of P, it is P-equivariant. So a continuous unanimous map, symmetric under P, exists exactly when g = 1, by Bézout. For full anonymity this is Chichilnisky's theorem (1980), proved independently by Eckmann (1954) as the non-existence of symmetric means on spheres. The gcd condition for products of symmetric groups is not in Chichilnisky (1996). Its Theorem 2 treats the group that permutes j given voters and fixes the others, under the assumption that the unanimous choices of these j voters reach every class at the level of homotopy groups ("j-full range"), and concludes that the space has no free homotopy. That assumption excludes the dictatorship of a voter outside the block, which is what the gcd condition allows when the other voters are alone. James (1956) studies symmetric maps of products of spheres by the degree on one factor (Chichilnisky's description; not read). With two blocks of two and three voters it exists: x₃x₄x₅ / (x₁x₂), of degrees (−1, −1, 1, 1, 1). *(Proved; elementary.)* With a Pareto condition the continuous case behaves like the discrete one of Section 3 (Chichilnisky 1982; in Lauwers' survey, every degree is 0 or 1 and exactly one is 1).

## 3. Positivity: the pinning lemma

For local Pareto rules, Proposition 1 is not sharp, because the degrees cannot be negative.

**Lemma 2 (pinning).** Every degree of a local Pareto rule is 0 or 1. *(Proved. The continuous counterpart is Chichilnisky's (1982) theorem as Baryshnikov states it: for continuous rules on spheres of linear preferences that respect unanimity on every comparison, exactly one voter is a "manipulator", who can produce any social preference whatever the others report; in Baryshnikov's terms, every voter is a homological dictator or homologically irrelevant.)*

*Proof.* By Lemma 1 the degree can be computed at any context. Let every other voter hold abc, and let voter i walk abc, bac, bca, cba, cab, acb and back. At bac all voters agree that a and b are above c, so Pareto puts the social ranking at abc or bac. The walk starts at abc, since the profile is unanimous. Its first step therefore goes forward or stands still. A degree of −1 would need six backward steps, and a degree of 2 or more would need more than six steps. ∎

The degree vector of a local Pareto rule is therefore a vertex of the simplex Σ dᵢ = 1, dᵢ ≥ 0, and P permutes these vertices as it permutes the voters.

**Proposition 3.** A local Pareto rule that is K-equivariant exists exactly when P fixes some voter. *(Proved: the degree-1 voter must form an orbit of P by itself, and that voter's dictatorship commutes with every element of K, since renamings and reversal act on the dictator's ballot as on the outcome.)*

This is stronger than Proposition 1. For blocks of two and three voters g = 1, so the torsion class vanishes, but no voter is fixed, and no such rule exists. The continuous example x₃x₄x₅ / (x₁x₂) has negative degrees, which Pareto excludes. *(Computed by 0/1 programs for blocks of sizes (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), (1, 1), (2, 1), (3, 1), (1, 1, 1), (2, 2), (2, 3) and (3, 3): rules exist exactly when there is a block of size one.)*

**Lotteries.** For a lottery rule whose real degree is defined (*The Global Form*, Section 4), and which is Paretian in Barberà and Sonnenschein's sense, the same context pins the real degree to [0, 1]. A joint draw along the loop starts and ends at abc with certainty, so every drawn path is closed, local and pinned, and has winding 0 or 1. The real degree is the expected winding. So the real degree vector lies in the simplex, and the barycentre of each orbit of P is a fixed point there. *(Proved. For the empirical models of Arrow's scenario Theorems 5 and 5′ of* Four Levels *give more, the mixture itself; the point here is that positivity needs only Pareto.)*

For strategy-proof lotteries the same context gives the open item of *The Global Form*, Section 12: a direct proof that unanimity makes the real degrees non-negative, without Gibbard (1977).

**Proposition 4.** Let a lottery rule for three alternatives be SD-strategy-proof and give the common top at identical ballots. Then every real degree lies in [0, 1]. *(Proved.)*

*Proof.* Let every other voter hold abc and voter i walk as in Lemma 2. Let α₁, …, α₆ ≥ 0 be the probabilities moved at the six steps, each in its permitted direction (the swap lemma of *The Global Form*, Section 7). Conservation of probability at a and at b gives

  D = α₁ − α₄ = α₃ − α₆ = α₅ − α₂,

at this context as at any other.
- **α₆ = 0.** The last step, acb to abc, moves probability from c to b. The profile with voter i at acb is reached from the unanimous profile at abc, where the lottery is a for certain, by raising c over b. That swap can move only probability that b holds, and b holds none. So c has probability 0 at acb.
- **α₂ = 0.** The second step, bac to bca, moves probability from a to c, at most what c holds at bca. The profile with voter i at bca is reached from the unanimous profile at bca, where the lottery is b for certain, by moving every other voter from bca to bac and then to abc. Each of these swaps raises a and moves probability only into a. So c keeps probability 0.

Hence D = α₃ ≥ 0, and D = α₁ − α₄ ≤ α₁ ≤ 1. ∎

The proof is the lottery form of Lemma 2: near the unanimous profile the outcome is pinned, and pinning excludes backward motion. With unanimity the real degrees therefore form a point of the simplex; Gibbard's theorem then identifies the rule as a mixture of dictatorships with these weights. Without unanimity the random top two has degree −½ (*The Global Form*, Proposition 4): it is the pinning that unanimity supplies and the random top two lacks.

## 4. Rigidity: what IIA adds

Lemma 2 makes some voter's degree 1. It does not make that voter a dictator.
- **Two voters.** Every local Pareto rule is a dictatorship: its output is always one of the two ballots, and if it follows voter 2 at abc against cba, it follows voter 2 everywhere. *(Computed.)*
- **Three voters.** There is a local Pareto rule with degrees (0, 0, 1) that differs from voter 3's ballot at 36 of the 216 profiles. *(Computed.)*

Turning degree 1 into a dictator takes IIA: Lemmas 3 and 4 of *The Global Form*, where a voter of non-zero degree is pivotal at every step and every context. Rigidity is not a homotopy-theoretic condition. It concerns which comparison may change at each step, not only how far the outcome moves.

For Gibbard–Satterthwaite all layers are carried by strategy-proofness. On the triangle of outcomes every rule is local, since any two outcomes are adjacent, so locality says nothing there. Strategy-proofness gives the squares (Lemma 5 of *The Global Form*), positivity and rigidity at once (Lemma 6), and Proposition 1 applies with the same group ℤ/g.

## 5. The ladder

Collected, for three alternatives:

| Layer | Condition | What fails there | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| stage 0 | Y^H ≠ ∅ at every profile | anonymous neutral rules for gcd(n, 6) > 1 (Moulin's condition, gcd(n, m!) = 1); anonymous reversal-symmetric rules for n even | arithmetic of n, pointwise |
| stage 1 | outcome at a midpoint fixed by its stabiliser | nothing; forces pivots at even splits (May at a tie) | pointwise, on ties |
| stage 2 | a class in H¹(X)^P restricting to 1 on Δ | every n ≥ 2 under anonymity, by ℤ/n; under P by ℤ/g (Chichilnisky's form) | torsion; vanishes over ℝ |
| positivity | degrees in {0, 1} (Pareto) or [0, 1] (lotteries) | symmetric rules unless P fixes a voter (Proposition 3) | order, not topology |
| rigidity | a voter of degree 1 is pivotal everywhere (IIA) | non-dictatorial rules (Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite) | combinatorial |

The three placements of *Where the Symmetry Goes* read off the ladder as follows.
- **(ii), a dictator.** The degree vector is a vertex of the simplex. Symmetry survives only as far as it fixes that vertex, so P must fix a voter.
- **(iii), a lottery.** The degree vector is any point of the simplex. Every P has fixed points there, the barycentres of its orbits, and full anonymity has one, 1/n. This is observation 3 of *Where the Symmetry Goes* made exact: the fixed point of the symmetry on the space of rules is the barycentre of the rules that break it. In stage 2 the same exit is the vanishing of torsion over ℝ.
- **(i), a paradox.** Replacing Y by a space whose loops contract removes stage 2: the Condorcet cycles as caps, or the weak orders with total indifference at the centre (*The Global Form*, Section 9). For the weak orders the fixed sets also become non-empty, since total indifference is fixed by every renaming and by reversal, so stage 0 disappears too. But an IIA rule that respects unanimity on every comparison never outputs a tie at a profile of strict ballots (Baryshnikov, Lemma 1). So the weak orders are an exit only for rules that give up IIA or Pareto, such as Borda. Pairwise majority takes the other exit, the caps. What is left is that the symmetric outputs are inadmissible, which is the case "A^H empty, Y^H non-empty" of the isotropy lemma.

The layers split between the two halves of the isotropy lemma's slogan "obstruction at points, obstruction along loops". Stages 0 and 1 are at points and need neutrality or reversal together with anonymity. Stage 2 is along loops and needs anonymity alone. Positivity and rigidity are not obstructions in the homotopy-theoretic sense at all. They are what makes the theorems with a dictator stronger than Chichilnisky's of 1980. His theorem of 1982, with Pareto, has positivity (one manipulator) but not rigidity. *(Interpretation of the propositions above.)*

## 6. More alternatives

For m alternatives the rankings form a sphere of dimension m − 2 (*The Global Form*, Section 3). The stages do not stop at dimension m − 1: the sphere has higher homotopy groups (π₃ of the 2-sphere is ℤ, for instance), and an equivariant map has to pass the obstructions they carry. One consequence of them can be read off cohomology directly: the cup square below.

The top stage has the same computation as for three alternatives. For IIA rules the class is supplied by Baryshnikov:
- the nerve of the cover of the strict orders by the sets "x above y" is a homotopy sphere of dimension m − 2 (his Theorem 1);
- the corresponding nerve of profiles has no cohomology below dimension m − 2 and has H^{m−2} = ℤⁿ, with basis pulled back from the n voters (his Propositions 4 and 5);
- an IIA rule is a simplicial map of the nerves, and the diagonal embedding sends the generator to the sum of the basis classes (his Lemma 3).

The voters' permutations permute that basis. Renamings and reversal act with the same sign on both sides (*The Global Form*, Section 9). So the top obstruction is again the class of 1 in ℤ/g. *(Proved for the top stage, from Baryshnikov's computation.)* Baryshnikov's Theorem 2 then combines this with his Proposition 7: every voter is a homological dictator (pairing 1) or homologically irrelevant (pairing 0). That is positivity and rigidity at once, both from IIA. Positivity for rules that are only local is not worked out; its natural form is Lemma 2 on each triple, which needs the rule to be local on triples, and that is IIA again.

**The cup square.** For continuous maps the class F*u of the generator u is more than an element of ℤⁿ. It lies in a ring, and since u² = 0 on the sphere, (F*u)² = 0 as well.

**Proposition 5.** Let the sphere of rankings have even dimension, that is, let m be even and at least 4. Every continuous map F from n copies of it to itself has at most one voter of non-zero degree. If F is unanimous, exactly one voter has degree 1 and all others have degree 0. So a unanimous map symmetric under a group of voters P exists exactly when P fixes some voter. *(Proved.)*

*Proof.* Let xᵢ be the generator pulled back from voter i's factor, so that F*u = Σ dᵢxᵢ. The squares xᵢ² vanish. In even degree the classes commute, so (F*u)² = 2 Σ_{i<j} dᵢdⱼ xᵢxⱼ, and the products xᵢxⱼ are independent by the Künneth theorem. So (F*u)² = 0 gives dᵢdⱼ = 0 for all i ≠ j. Unanimity makes the degrees sum to 1, so one of them is 1 and the others vanish. The degrees are constant on the orbits of P (Proposition 1), so that voter's orbit has a single member. Conversely, the dictatorship of a voter fixed by P is P-symmetric. ∎

This is the classical cup-product argument that even-dimensional spheres admit no multiplication. For rules it says that, from four alternatives on and for every even number of them, a continuous unanimous rule has a homological dictator without Pareto, neutrality or IIA. Neither the gcd of Proposition 1 nor any symmetry of the alternatives plays a part. *(The first statement is classical; the consequences are proved.)*

For odd dimension the cup square vanishes by itself, because classes of odd degree anticommute. The obstruction then sits one level higher, in the Whitehead square [ι, ι] of the identity of the sphere. A map from the product of two copies of the sphere to the sphere with degrees a and b on the two factors exists exactly when ab·[ι, ι] = 0. In odd dimension [ι, ι] has order 2, except in dimensions 1, 3 and 7, where the sphere carries a multiplication and [ι, ι] = 0; that the exceptions are exactly these is Adams' theorem on the Hopf invariant.†

**Proposition 5′.** Let the sphere of rankings have odd dimension other than 1, 3 and 7, that is, let m be odd, m ≥ 7 and m ≠ 9. In every continuous map from n copies of it to itself, any two voters' degrees have an even product. If the map is unanimous, exactly one voter has odd degree. So a unanimous map symmetric under a group of voters P exists exactly when P fixes some voter. *(Proved from the classical facts just stated.)*

*Proof.* Fix all voters but i and j at a base point. The resulting map of two factors has degrees dᵢ and dⱼ, so dᵢdⱼ[ι, ι] = 0, and dᵢdⱼ is even. The degrees sum to 1, so at least one is odd, and by the pairwise condition at most one. The degrees are constant on the orbits of P, so the voter of odd degree is alone in its orbit. Conversely, that voter's dictatorship is P-symmetric. ∎

So the only numbers of alternatives from four on at which a symmetric continuous rule without a voter fixed by the symmetry is not excluded by homotopy theory alone are five and nine, where the sphere of rankings is the group of unit quaternions or the unit octonions. For three alternatives the circle admits the monomials of Section 2. *(Proved.)*

**Fixed sets.** For symmetries of the alternatives, take the sphere of rankings in its realised form: unit utility vectors with coordinate sum zero, in a space V of dimension m − 1. A strict ranking is an open chamber, and a ballot with ties lies on the walls. Renamings permute the coordinates and reversal is −1, so the fixed set of an element is the unit sphere of an eigenspace:
- a renaming π fixes the vectors that are constant on each cycle of π. Its fixed set is a sphere of dimension c(π) − 2, where c(π) is the number of cycles. It consists of the ballots in which each cycle is one tie block, so it is the sphere of rankings of a problem with c(π) alternatives, one per cycle;
- reversal composed with π fixes the (−1)-eigenspace of π, of dimension e(π), the number of cycles of even length. Its fixed set is a sphere of dimension e(π) − 1.

*(Proved; the dimensions checked for every element with m ≤ 6.)* For three alternatives this gives the list of Section 2: a transposition fixes two midpoints, and reversal composed with a transposition fixes two rankings. For m alternatives a transposition fixes a sphere of dimension m − 3, the rankings of the m − 1 blocks {a, b}, c, d, …. For odd m this sphere has even dimension, and Proposition 5 applies on it.

**The strata.** Each stratum is a problem of the same kind with fewer alternatives and composite voters, so the ladder of Sections 2 and 5 recurs on it, for any m.

**Proposition 6 (strata).** Let h = (σ, γ) be in K, and let k be the dimension of the fixed space V^γ, so that Y^γ is a sphere of dimension k − 1. The stratum X^h is a product with one factor per cycle of σ: a cycle of length ℓ contributes the fixed sphere of γ^ℓ, through the ballot of any one of its voters. Call a cycle short if γ^ℓ fixes no more than γ. A class of short cycles is an orbit of the elements of K that normalise ⟨h⟩; for a product of symmetric groups on blocks it consists of the cycles of one length within one block. Then a continuous unanimous K-equivariant map has to pass the following condition on X^h. *(Proved.)*
- **k = 0.** The stratum must be empty: some cycle has γ^ℓ without fixed vectors.
- **k = 1.** Y^γ is two points, and the components of X^h are labelled by the points of the short factors. There must be at least one short cycle. And no element of K normalising ⟨h⟩ may reverse the line V^γ while sending some labelling to its negative. For products of symmetric groups this means that some class of short cycles has odd size: May's condition at a tie.
- **k ≥ 2.** There must be a short cycle, and the gcd g_h of the sizes of the classes of short cycles must be 1. The obstruction is the class of 1 in ℤ/g_h, or in ℤ if there is no short cycle.

*Proof for k ≥ 2.* The cohomology of X^h in degree k − 1 has one generator per short cycle. Long cycles contribute spheres of higher dimension, and products of positive classes have higher degree. The unanimous profiles at points of Y^γ lie in X^h; the diagonal sends the generator to the sum over the short cycles, so the degrees on the short cycles sum to 1. Let an element (ρ, β) of K normalise ⟨h⟩. It maps X^h to itself and permutes the cycles of σ, and β preserves Y^γ with some degree ε = ±1, acting by β on each short factor. Equivariance then gives ε·d_{ρ(c)} = ε·d_c for every short cycle c: the sign of the renaming part acts on both sides and cancels, as in Proposition 1. So the degrees are constant on classes, and Σ (class size) · d = 1 needs g_h = 1. The cases k = 0 and k = 1 are the same argument in degrees −1 and 0. ∎

For example, let five voters form blocks {1, 2} and {3, 4, 5}, let τ be the transposition of a and b, and let h swap voters 1 and 2 and apply τ. The swap is a long cycle, since τ² = 1, and the three fixed voters form one class of short cycles, so the stratum carries ℤ/3 although the top stage vanishes (gcd(2, 3) = 1). On this stratum voters 1 and 2 have become one composite voter whose ballot moves on a sphere of higher dimension and carries no degree. The other three face a problem with the m − 1 alternatives {a, b}, c, d, … and anonymity among them. For three alternatives the same stratum is stage 1, with values in two midpoints, and the condition is May's: an odd number of remaining voters. *(Proved; for m = 4 the conclusion also follows from Proposition 5, without neutrality.)*

Proposition 6 is not the whole obstruction theory for groups that are not products. The strata also have to agree where they meet: the degrees of a stratum restricted to a smaller stratum inside it must be the degrees of the smaller one. Let P act as S₃ on voters 1, 2, 3 and, through the sign, on voters 0 and 4, so that a transposition of 1, 2, 3 also swaps 0 and 4. No single stratum obstructs, but the strata of (1, τ) and of ((04)(12), τ) meet in the profiles with u₄ = u₀, u₂ = u₁ and every ballot fixed by τ. Restricted from the first they have degrees 2α, 2β and β, where α and β are the degrees of the first stratum with 2α + 3β = 1. Restricted from the second they have degrees 0, 0 and d₃, because the pairs are long cycles there. So β = 0, against 2α + 3β = 1. *(Proved; for m = 4 Proposition 5 gives the same conclusion directly.)*

**Theorem 7.** Let m ≥ 4, and let a continuous unanimous map be symmetric under a group of voters P. If m is 5 or 9, let it also be equivariant under the renamings in a group that contains a transposition, with the voters' permutations and the renamings acting independently (K = P × Γ′). Then the map exists exactly when P fixes some voter, and every such map has a voter fixed by P whose degree is 1 (for even m) or odd (for odd m). *(Proved.)*

*Proof.* For even m this is Proposition 5, and for odd m other than 5 and 9 Proposition 5′. For m = 5 or 9, let τ be the transposition. The profiles in which every ballot is fixed by τ form the stratum (S^{m−3})ⁿ, where S^{m−3} is the fixed sphere of τ, and equivariance sends it into S^{m−3}. The restriction is unanimous on the stratum, and the voters' permutations in P commute with τ, so the restriction is P-symmetric. Its sphere has even dimension m − 3 ≥ 2, so Proposition 5 gives a voter fixed by P. The dictatorship of a voter fixed by P is K-equivariant. ∎

So from four alternatives on, continuous rules leave only dictatorships of voters that the symmetry fixes. For an even number of alternatives this comes from the cup square, for an odd number other than five and nine from the Whitehead square. For five and nine neutrality is needed, and it brings the cup square onto the fixed sphere of a transposition; there neutrality does for continuous maps what Pareto does for local rules with three alternatives (Proposition 3). With three alternatives the theorem fails: a transposition fixes only two points, and the neutral map x₃x₄x₅ / (x₁x₂) on the circle exists, since rotations and reflections of the circle commute with it.

**Five and nine alternatives without neutrality.** Here the question becomes one about symmetric maps of spheres. Call j_k the least positive degree on one factor (the James number) of a symmetric map from k copies of the sphere to itself; symmetric maps with James number q exist exactly for the multiples of j_k, since the pointwise product of two symmetric maps is symmetric with the sum of the James numbers, and composing with the inverse changes the sign.

**Proposition 8.** Let the sphere of rankings be S³ or S⁷ (m = 5 or 9), and let P be a product of symmetric groups on blocks B₁, …, B_r of voters. A continuous unanimous P-symmetric map exists exactly when the numbers |B_t| · j_{|B_t|} have greatest common divisor 1. *(Proved.)*

*Proof.* If such a map exists, fixing every voter outside block B_t at a base point gives a symmetric map of |B_t| variables, so the common degree d_t of the block's voters is a multiple of j_{|B_t|}; unanimity gives Σ |B_t| d_t = 1. Conversely, given such d_t, take symmetric maps f_t of the blocks with James numbers d_t and multiply them pointwise in a fixed order. The product is P-symmetric, and on the diagonal its degree is Σ |B_t| d_t = 1, since the diagonal of a block is homologous to the sum of its axes and degrees add under the multiplication. A map homotopic to the identity on the diagonal can be made equal to it there by the equivariant homotopy extension property, keeping it P-symmetric. ∎

For blocks of two and three voters on S³ this asks whether 2j₂ and 3j₃ are coprime. The values of j_k for S³ and S⁷ are studied by James, Ucci and Crabb and Knapp, and were not available here. *(Open.)*

**Computations.** For three and four alternatives and up to eight voters, with every block structure and with renamings, reversal or both, the primary obstructions of Proposition 6 were computed. With neutrality and m = 4 the top stage vanishes but a stratum obstructs exactly for the blocks (3, 2), (5, 2), (4, 3), (3, 2, 2), (5, 3) and (3, 3, 2), the structures with gcd 1 and no block of size 1. With reversal alone no stratum obstructs, but for m = 4 Proposition 5 rules these structures out anyway. Over the permutation groups up to seven voters that were sampled, with neutrality and m = 4, every group without a fixed voter is obstructed once the strata are compared with each other, and every group with a fixed voter tested is not. *(Computed.)*

**Literature.** Juárez-Anguiano (2024) lets a group act on the preference space, which includes neutrality, and restricts the rule to its fixed sets, but only with full anonymity, so only the strata of elements that rename without permuting voters occur. Chichilnisky (1996) treats the permutations of one block of voters under a full-range condition on that block, without a group on the preferences. Strata of elements that permute voters and rename at once were not found in either, nor in Lauwers' survey (2000). The cup-product and Whitehead-product arguments are classical; their consequence for rules on spheres of rankings with partial anonymity was not found stated in these sources, but it is elementary enough that it may be known.

For rules with positivity the lower strata add nothing to existence. For local Pareto rules with three alternatives (Proposition 3), for unanimous IIA rules with any number (Arrow's theorem), and for continuous rules with an even number of alternatives (Proposition 5), a K-equivariant rule exists exactly when P fixes a voter, and then that voter's dictatorship satisfies every stage. *(Proved.)*

## 7. Relation to the electorate direction

**The continuous no-show paradox.** Chèze proves, for continuous rules on spheres, that a metric participation condition cannot hold: adding a voter must move the outcome weakly towards that voter's preference. A twin condition of the same kind cannot hold either. His proof uses neither anonymity nor unanimity. The condition keeps the slice in which two voters i and j hold the same preference x, with the others fixed, away from −x. So that slice is homotopic to the identity, and its degree d_i + d_j is 1 for every pair. With three voters this forces d_i = ½, which is not an integer. In the terms of this note the obstruction is again a torsion class of order 2 at the top stage, with real solution ½ for everyone. The part played by the group in Proposition 1, making degrees equal, is played by the pairwise conditions. *(Chèze's theorem; the reading is interpretation.)* His condition is metric and differs from ranking-based participation, which three alternatives can satisfy for finite electorates (maximin, *Consistency as an Extension Problem*, Section 3c). So the continuous and the finite no-show theorems are not the same statement.

**The finite statements.** Young–Levenglick and the no-show theorem for finite electorates do not fit this ladder. No group is involved in their obstruction. At a profile with a majority cycle every candidate is blocked separately (*The Global Form*, Section 10), and the obstruction is a failure of admissibility, not of equivariance. In *Four Levels* they sit at level 1, below contextuality, while everything in this note concerns level 3 and its division to level 2. An obstruction-theoretic statement for them belongs to thread A. Its form is a certificate of infeasibility of the linear program in *Consistency as an Extension Problem*, Section 3c, rather than a cohomology class, and for Young–Levenglick with three alternatives the certificates are worked out there in Section 3d. *(Interpretation.)*

## 8. Open questions

1. More alternatives. For continuous rules Theorem 7 leaves only dictatorships of voters fixed by the symmetry: by the cup square for even m, by the Whitehead square for odd m other than 5 and 9, and with neutrality for 5 and 9. Open: five and nine alternatives without neutrality, which Proposition 8 reduces to the James numbers of symmetric maps of S³ and S⁷; a complete obstruction theory for strata of groups that are not products, with the restriction maps between strata; and discrete (local) rules for m ≥ 4, where the strata are subcomplexes of the permutohedron.
2. An equivariant form of positivity. The degree vector lies in a cone fixed by Pareto, and the obstruction of Proposition 3 is the absence of a fixed vertex. Is there a formulation in which positivity is a coefficient condition, so that the ladder becomes one obstruction theory with ordered coefficients?
3. For which local Pareto rules is the degree-1 voter a dictator? Two voters always; three voters not. Is there a condition weaker than IIA that suffices, such as locality on every pair of comparisons?
