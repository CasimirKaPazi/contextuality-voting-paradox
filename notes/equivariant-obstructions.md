# Equivariant Obstructions: the Stages of the Symmetry Problem

*Working note for thread B of the roadmap. It writes the symmetry problem of aggregation, for three alternatives, as an extension problem for equivariant maps and takes it apart in the order of equivariant obstruction theory. The isotropy lemma of* Where the Symmetry Goes *is the obstruction at points. Ties give a condition at the midpoints of steps. The degree of* The Global Form *is the last obstruction, and it is a torsion class of order n. Two layers follow that are not homotopy-theoretic: the Pareto condition pins every degree to 0 or 1, and independence of irrelevant alternatives turns degree 1 into a dictator. The three placements then become statements about this ladder: a dictator is a vertex of the simplex of degree vectors fixed by the symmetry, a lottery is its barycentre, and a paradox is a target space whose loops contract. Proved statements, computed checks and interpretation are marked. Checks:* `scripts/symmetry/equivariant_stages.py`.

## 1. Setting

Take three alternatives. As in *The Global Form*, the six rankings form a hexagon Y, two rankings being adjacent when they differ in one comparison. Profiles of n voters form the product X = Yⁿ, a complex of cubes: a vertex is a profile, an edge is a step of one voter, a square is a step of each of two voters, and so on. The diagonal Δ ⊂ X consists of the unanimous profiles and is a copy of Y.

**Symmetries.** The group G = Sₙ × D₆ acts on X:
- the voters' permutations Sₙ permute the factors and act trivially on Y;
- the dihedral group D₆, of order 12, consists of the renamings of the alternatives (S₃) and reversal. It acts on every factor and on Y.

On the hexagon the renamings act as follows. A 3-cycle is a rotation by two steps. A transposition is a reflection through the midpoints of two opposite edges. Reversal is the half rotation, and reversal composed with a transposition is a reflection through two opposite vertices. Only the last kind fixes a ranking.

**Rules.** A rule F sends profiles to rankings. Two conditions take the place of independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) for most of the note.
- F is **local** if a step of one voter moves the social ranking by at most one step on the hexagon.
- F is **Pareto** if it respects every comparison on which all voters agree.

Every IIA rule is local (*The Global Form*, Section 1), and every unanimous IIA rule into rankings is Pareto. Lemma 1 of *The Global Form* uses only locality: around a square the social ranking takes at most four steps, which cannot go around the hexagon. So a local rule has a degree deg_i for every voter, independent of the context, and the vector d = (deg₁, …, degₙ) is the class of F in H¹(X; ℤ) = ℤⁿ. Lemma 2 there uses IIA only on the staircase, where every voter agrees on the two comparisons that the joint step leaves alone. Pareto keeps the social ranking on the edge between the two diagonal rankings, which is all the lemma needs. So for a local Pareto rule the degrees sum to 1. *(Proved.)*

A symmetry condition is a subgroup K of G, and F is K-equivariant if F(k·x) = k·F(x). Anonymity is K = Sₙ, neutrality K = S₃, reversal symmetry the subgroup of order 2 generated by reversal, and any combination or any subgroup in between is allowed. Write P for the image of K in Sₙ. For example, a rule may be symmetric only within blocks of voters, or only under swapping two voters together with reversing all ballots.

**Subdivision.** Equivariant obstruction theory asks that a cell mapped to itself by a group element be fixed pointwise. A transposition of alternatives can swap the two ends of a step, and a permutation of voters can reflect a square, so the hexagon and X are subdivided barycentrically. The midpoint of a step from r to r′ is the ballot that is indifferent on the pair the step flips, a weak order with one tie. So the new vertices of X are the profiles in which some voters are indifferent on one pair, and the midpoints of Y are the six weak orders with one tie.

## 2. The stages

For an equivariant map into Y, the extension over a cell whose stabiliser is H meets an obstruction in π_{k−1}(Y^H) at dimension k, where Y^H is the part of Y that H fixes (Bredon; tom Dieck, Section II.3†). For the subdivided hexagon, Y^H is:
- all of Y, a circle, if H acts trivially on Y, that is, if H only permutes voters;
- two opposite rankings, for a vertex reflection;
- two opposite midpoints, for an edge reflection;
- empty otherwise.

So the stages are 0, 1 and 2. Since π_k of a circle vanishes for k ≥ 2, nothing beyond stage 2 can obstruct.

### Stage 0: points

At a profile x with stabiliser H, F(x) must lie in Y^H. This is the isotropy lemma of *Where the Symmetry Goes*, Section 7. Without anonymity it never obstructs, because a stabiliser that permutes no voters fixes every ballot, so it fixes some ranking. With anonymity:

| Symmetry | Stage 0 fails for | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| anonymity and neutrality | gcd(n, 6) > 1 | for 2 dividing n, a profile of pairs r, τr for a transposition τ; for 3, the Condorcet profile |
| anonymity and reversal | n even | a profile of pairs r, reverse of r |
| anonymity, neutrality and reversal | gcd(n, 6) > 1 | as the first row |
| single winners, anonymity and neutrality | 3 dividing n | Moulin's condition for three alternatives |

*(Proved for "fails"; computed for "only then", n ≤ 10.)* These are the arithmetic conditions recovered in *Where the Symmetry Goes*, Section 7. They depend on n through its prime factors up to the number of alternatives.

### Stage 1: midpoints

Let a step of one voter go from the profile x, with ballot r, to x′, with ballot r′, and let an element of K swap its two ends. On the hexagon it swaps r and r′, so it is the transposition τ of the pair the step flips, and it must also fix the multiset of the other ballots (with anonymity; without it, every other ballot, which is possible only for one voter). Then F(x′) = τF(x). Since τ fixes no ranking, F(x′) ≠ F(x), and by locality the two are adjacent, on an edge that τ maps to itself. Such an edge flips the same pair: the moving voter is **pivotal** there. On the subdivision this is stage 0 at the midpoint x̄, the profile in which the voter is indifferent on the pair: F(x̄) must be a midpoint fixed by τ. *(Proved.)*

This is May's theorem at a tie. The other voters are split evenly on the pair, as τ requires, and a neutral rule must let the last voter decide. It does not obstruct: dictatorships satisfy it, and Y^H is never empty at a midpoint. But it forces pivots:
- with anonymity and neutrality, at the steps where the other ballots come in pairs r, τr. There are none for n even and some for n odd, for example 18 of the 126 steps for three voters;
- with neutrality and one voter, at every step. The outcome then moves at every step. Two rankings two steps apart are carried into each other by a 3-cycle, which fixes no ranking, so their outcomes differ and the outcome never steps back. Its degree is ±1. In fact only the dictatorship and the anti-dictatorship are local, both of degree 1. *(Proved; the last statement computed.)*

### Stage 2: the degree as a torsion class

Unanimity prescribes F on Δ: the identity of Y. The question is whether it extends to an equivariant map on X, and the last obstruction is the degree.

**Proposition 1.** Let F be a local, Pareto and K-equivariant rule, and let g be the greatest common divisor of the sizes of the orbits of P on the voters. Then the degree vector is constant on the orbits of P and sums to 1. Integer vectors with both properties exist exactly when g = 1, so the rule can exist only then. The obstruction is the class of 1 in ℤ/g. *(Proved.)*

*Proof.*
- **Constant on orbits.** Let k = (σ, γ) be in K. The map k acts on H¹(X) by permuting the coordinates by σ and multiplying them by the degree χ(γ) = ±1 of γ on the hexagon, and γ acts on H¹(Y) by the same χ(γ). Equivariance gives k*[F] = F*γ*[Y], so χ(γ)·σd = χ(γ)·d, and σd = d.
- **The sum.** The restriction H¹(X) → H¹(Δ) is d ↦ Σ deg_i, and unanimity requires 1. On vectors constant on the orbits O, the sum is Σ_O |O|·d_O, whose values form gℤ. ∎

The renamings and reversal drop out of the proof because they act with the same sign on both sides, as in *The Global Form*, Proposition 6. Only P matters. It is the image of K and not its intersection with Sₙ, so a symmetry that swaps two voters and reverses all ballots at the same time counts as swapping the voters.

**In the language of obstruction theory.** For K = P, an equivariant map is a map from X/P to Y. Here X/P is a product of symmetric products of the hexagon, one per block, and each symmetric product of a circle is homotopy equivalent to a circle. Morton shows that the n-th symmetric product is a bundle of (n − 1)-simplices over a circle, orientable exactly for n odd. His proof also shows the arithmetic directly: one voter going once around moves the base by 1/n of a turn, so the diagonal loop is n times the generator. So H¹(X/P) = ℤ^b for b blocks, and its restriction to Δ is (d_j) ↦ Σ_j |block j|·d_j. The exact sequence of the pair (X/P, Δ) gives H²(X/P, Δ; ℤ) ≅ ℤ/g ⊕ H²(X/P), with H²(X/P) free. The primary obstruction to extending the unanimous map is the image of 1 under the connecting map. It is the generator of the torsion part ℤ/g. *(Proved.)*

**Torsion and lotteries.** With real coefficients ℤ/g ⊗ ℝ = 0, and the obstruction vanishes. With rational coefficients it vanishes as well, and ℤ[1/g] suffices. For full anonymity the solution is d_i = 1/n, random dictatorship, whose probabilities are multiples of 1/n, exactly the denominators the torsion requires. Placement (iii) is possible because the global obstruction is torsion. Convexification kills torsion. *(The first statements proved; the reading of placement (iii) is interpretation.)*

**For continuous maps without Pareto the stage is sharp.** On circles, the map (x₁, …, xₙ) ↦ x₁^{a₁} ⋯ xₙ^{aₙ}, in complex notation, is unanimous exactly when Σ aᵢ = 1. If the aᵢ are constant on the orbits of P, it is P-equivariant. So a continuous unanimous map, symmetric under P, exists exactly when g = 1, by Bézout. For full anonymity this is Chichilnisky's theorem (1980). With two blocks of two and three voters it exists: x₃x₄x₅ / (x₁x₂), of degrees (−1, −1, 1, 1, 1). *(Proved; elementary.)* With a Pareto condition the continuous case behaves like the discrete one of Section 3 (Chichilnisky 1982).

## 3. Positivity: the pinning lemma

For local Pareto rules, Proposition 1 is not sharp, because the degrees cannot be negative.

**Lemma 2 (pinning).** Every degree of a local Pareto rule is 0 or 1. *(Proved. The continuous counterpart is Chichilnisky's (1982) theorem as Baryshnikov states it: for continuous rules on spheres of linear preferences that respect unanimity on every comparison, exactly one voter is a "manipulator", who can produce any social preference whatever the others report; in Baryshnikov's terms, every voter is a homological dictator or homologically irrelevant.)*

*Proof.* By Lemma 1 the degree can be computed at any context. Let every other voter hold abc, and let voter i walk abc, bac, bca, cba, cab, acb and back. At bac all voters agree that a and b are above c, so Pareto puts the social ranking at abc or bac. The walk starts at abc, since the profile is unanimous. Its first step therefore goes forward or stands still. A degree of −1 would need six backward steps, and a degree of 2 or more would need more than six steps. ∎

The degree vector of a local Pareto rule is therefore a vertex of the simplex Σ dᵢ = 1, dᵢ ≥ 0, and P permutes these vertices as it permutes the voters.

**Proposition 3.** A local Pareto rule that is K-equivariant exists exactly when P fixes some voter. *(Proved: the degree-1 voter must form an orbit of P by itself, and that voter's dictatorship commutes with every element of K, since renamings and reversal act on the dictator's ballot as on the outcome.)*

This is stronger than Proposition 1. For blocks of two and three voters g = 1, so the torsion class vanishes, but no voter is fixed, and no such rule exists. The continuous example x₃x₄x₅ / (x₁x₂) has negative degrees, which Pareto excludes. *(Computed by 0/1 programs for blocks of sizes (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), (1, 1), (2, 1), (3, 1), (1, 1, 1), (2, 2), (2, 3) and (3, 3): rules exist exactly when there is a block of size one.)*

**Lotteries.** For a lottery rule whose real degree is defined (*The Global Form*, Section 4), and which is Paretian in Barberà and Sonnenschein's sense, the same context pins the real degree to [0, 1]. A joint draw along the loop starts and ends at abc with certainty, so every drawn path is closed, local and pinned, and has winding 0 or 1. The real degree is the expected winding. So the real degree vector lies in the simplex, and the barycentre of each orbit of P is a fixed point there. *(Proved. For the empirical models of Arrow's scenario Theorems 5 and 5′ of* Four Levels *give more, the mixture itself; the point here is that positivity needs only Pareto.)*

For strategy-proof lotteries the same context gives the open item of *The Global Form*, Section 12: a direct proof that unanimity makes the real degrees non-negative, without Gibbard (1977).

**Proposition 4.** Let a lottery rule for three alternatives be SD-strategy-proof and give the common top at identical ballots. Then every real degree lies in [0, 1]. *(Proved.)*

*Proof.* Let every other voter hold abc and voter i walk as in Lemma 2. Let α₁, …, α₆ ≥ 0 be the probabilities moved at the six steps, each in its permitted direction (the swap lemma of *The Global Form*, Section 7). Conservation of probability at a and at b gives

  D = α₁ − α₄ = α₃ − α₆ = α₅ − α₂,

at this context as at any other.
- **α₆ = 0.** The last step, acb to abc, moves probability from c to b. The profile with voter i at acb is reached from the unanimous profile at abc, where the lottery is a for certain, by raising c over b. That swap can move only probability that b holds, and b holds none. So c has probability 0 at acb.
- **α₂ = 0.** The second step, bac to bca, moves probability from a to c, at most what c holds at bca. The profile with voter i at bca is reached from the unanimous profile at bca, where the lottery is b for certain, by moving every other voter from bca to bac and then to abc. Each of these swaps raises a and moves probability only into a. So c keeps probability 0.

Hence D = α₃ ≥ 0, and D = α₁ − α₄ ≤ α₁ ≤ 1. ∎

The proof is the lottery form of Lemma 2: near the unanimous profile the outcome is pinned, and pinning excludes backward motion. With unanimity the real degrees therefore form a point of the simplex; Gibbard's theorem then identifies the rule as a mixture of dictatorships with these weights. Without unanimity the random top two has degree −½ (*The Global Form*, Proposition 4): it is the pinning that unanimity supplies and the random top two lacks.

## 4. Rigidity: what IIA adds

Lemma 2 makes some voter's degree 1. It does not make that voter a dictator.
- **Two voters.** Every local Pareto rule is a dictatorship: its output is always one of the two ballots, and if it follows voter 2 at abc against cba, it follows voter 2 everywhere. *(Computed.)*
- **Three voters.** There is a local Pareto rule with degrees (0, 0, 1) that differs from voter 3's ballot at 36 of the 216 profiles. *(Computed.)*

Turning degree 1 into a dictator takes IIA: Lemmas 3 and 4 of *The Global Form*, where a voter of non-zero degree is pivotal at every step and every context. Rigidity is not a homotopy-theoretic condition. It concerns which comparison may change at each step, not only how far the outcome moves.

For Gibbard–Satterthwaite all layers are carried by strategy-proofness. On the triangle of outcomes every rule is local, since any two outcomes are adjacent, so locality says nothing there. Strategy-proofness gives the squares (Lemma 5 of *The Global Form*), positivity and rigidity at once (Lemma 6), and Proposition 1 applies with the same group ℤ/g.

## 5. The ladder

Collected, for three alternatives:

| Layer | Condition | What fails there | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| stage 0 | Y^H ≠ ∅ at every profile | anonymous neutral rules for gcd(n, 6) > 1 (Moulin's condition, gcd(n, m!) = 1); anonymous reversal-symmetric rules for n even | arithmetic of n, pointwise |
| stage 1 | outcome at a midpoint fixed by its stabiliser | nothing; forces pivots at even splits (May at a tie) | pointwise, on ties |
| stage 2 | a class in H¹(X)^P restricting to 1 on Δ | every n ≥ 2 under anonymity, by ℤ/n; under P by ℤ/g (Chichilnisky's form) | torsion; vanishes over ℝ |
| positivity | degrees in {0, 1} (Pareto) or [0, 1] (lotteries) | symmetric rules unless P fixes a voter (Proposition 3) | order, not topology |
| rigidity | a voter of degree 1 is pivotal everywhere (IIA) | non-dictatorial rules (Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite) | combinatorial |

The three placements of *Where the Symmetry Goes* read off the ladder as follows.
- **(ii), a dictator.** The degree vector is a vertex of the simplex. Symmetry survives only as far as it fixes that vertex, so P must fix a voter.
- **(iii), a lottery.** The degree vector is any point of the simplex. Every P has fixed points there, the barycentres of its orbits, and full anonymity has one, 1/n. This is observation 3 of *Where the Symmetry Goes* made exact: the fixed point of the symmetry on the space of rules is the barycentre of the rules that break it. In stage 2 the same exit is the vanishing of torsion over ℝ.
- **(i), a paradox.** Replacing Y by a space whose loops contract removes stage 2: the Condorcet cycles as caps, or the weak orders with total indifference at the centre (*The Global Form*, Section 9). For the weak orders the fixed sets also become non-empty, since total indifference is fixed by every renaming and by reversal, so stage 0 disappears too. But an IIA rule that respects unanimity on every comparison never outputs a tie at a profile of strict ballots (Baryshnikov, Lemma 1). So the weak orders are an exit only for rules that give up IIA or Pareto, such as Borda. Pairwise majority takes the other exit, the caps. What is left is that the symmetric outputs are inadmissible, which is the case "A^H empty, Y^H non-empty" of the isotropy lemma.

The layers split between the two halves of the isotropy lemma's slogan "obstruction at points, obstruction along loops". Stages 0 and 1 are at points and need neutrality or reversal together with anonymity. Stage 2 is along loops and needs anonymity alone. Positivity and rigidity are not obstructions in the homotopy-theoretic sense at all. They are what makes the theorems with a dictator stronger than Chichilnisky's of 1980. His theorem of 1982, with Pareto, has positivity (one manipulator) but not rigidity. *(Interpretation of the propositions above.)*

## 6. More alternatives

For m alternatives the rankings form a sphere of dimension m − 2 (*The Global Form*, Section 3), and stages run up to m − 1. The top stage has the same computation. For IIA rules the class is supplied by Baryshnikov:
- the nerve of the cover of the strict orders by the sets "x above y" is a homotopy sphere of dimension m − 2 (his Theorem 1);
- the corresponding nerve of profiles has no cohomology below dimension m − 2 and has H^{m−2} = ℤⁿ, with basis pulled back from the n voters (his Propositions 4 and 5);
- an IIA rule is a simplicial map of the nerves, and the diagonal embedding sends the generator to the sum of the basis classes (his Lemma 3).

The voters' permutations permute that basis. Renamings and reversal act with the same sign on both sides (*The Global Form*, Section 9). So the top obstruction is again the class of 1 in ℤ/g. *(Proved for the top stage, from Baryshnikov's computation.)* Baryshnikov's Theorem 2 then combines this with his Proposition 7: every voter is a homological dictator (pairing 1) or homologically irrelevant (pairing 0). That is positivity and rigidity at once, both from IIA. Not worked out:
- the lower stages, where the fixed sets of renamings are spheres of lower dimension inside the sphere of utility vectors;
- positivity, whose natural form is Lemma 2 on each triple. It needs the rule to be local on triples, which is IIA again.

## 7. Relation to the electorate direction

Young–Levenglick and the no-show theorem do not fit this ladder. No group is involved in their obstruction. At a profile with a majority cycle every candidate is blocked separately (*The Global Form*, Section 10), and the obstruction is a failure of admissibility, not of equivariance. In *Four Levels* they sit at level 1, below contextuality, while everything in this note concerns level 3 and its division to level 2. An obstruction-theoretic statement for them belongs to thread A, and its natural form is a certificate of infeasibility of the linear program in *Consistency as an Extension Problem*, Section 3c, rather than a cohomology class. *(Interpretation.)*

## 8. Open questions

1. The lower stages for m ≥ 4, and whether any of them obstructs beyond the arithmetic of stage 0.
2. An equivariant form of positivity. The degree vector lies in a cone fixed by Pareto, and the obstruction of Proposition 3 is the absence of a fixed vertex. Is there a formulation in which positivity is a coefficient condition, so that the ladder becomes one obstruction theory with ordered coefficients?
3. For which local Pareto rules is the degree-1 voter a dictator? Two voters always; three voters not. Is there a condition weaker than IIA that suffices, such as locality on every pair of comparisons?
