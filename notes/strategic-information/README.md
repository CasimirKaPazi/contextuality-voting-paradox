## 1. The question

The impossibility theorems of Arrow and Gibbard describe the upper end of the space of voting methods: once a method is good enough, its remaining defects are trade-offs, and no method has all the properties at once. They say nothing about methods that lie below that frontier. A method can be worse than another without any trade-off, if the other does at least as well in every respect that both are judged by. The aim of these notes is to make such comparisons precise for methods that are similar enough to be compared pair by pair.

The comparison has two steps. The first asks for the best use a method can make of the information in the electorate under ideal assumptions about the voters. The second asks for the worst outcome under the same assumptions. If one method is at least as good as another in both, and better in one, the other lies below the frontier. Neither step compares sincere ballots: voters who leave gains on the table are not the point. Both methods are played by voters who follow the same rationale, and the rationale must be stated without reference to the method. *The Common Rationale*, Section 1, says how far this is achieved: one model of beliefs for all methods away from the corners, and a separate assumption for each method in its corner.

This is one of two axes of comparison. The other is what a method achieves when every voter is honest, judged by total utility; it is the ideal case, and the strategic comparison describes where voters who respond to polls move away from it (Section 5.2).

## 2. Information in two stages

The information a method can use is described in two stages: what its ballots and its count can carry, which depends on the method alone, and what they carry when voters fill in the ballots strategically.

**Stage 1: what the method can carry.** This has two parts.

- *The ballot space* B, the set of ballots a voter may cast. With m candidates, plurality has m ballots (one name), approval 2^m (any set of names), score with scores 0 to k has (k + 1)^m, strict rankings m!, and rankings that allow equal ranks (weak orders) have the ordered Bell numbers, 13 for three candidates and 75 for four. A single ballot can tell at most log₂|B| bits.
- *The count* τ. A profile of ballots is recorded by its histogram, the number of voters who cast each ballot. A statistic of the profile is *additive* if its value on two electorates together is the sum of its values on each: totals, pairwise counts and the histogram itself are additive. The count of a method is the coarsest additive statistic that determines its outcome: two profiles Q and Q′ have the same count exactly when the method gives p + Q and p + Q′ the same outcome for every profile p. *(Definition. Ties are left aside here; the working note* Equality of Voters and Alternatives *treats them, and calls a profile settled if no ballot together with its reverse changes the outcome from it on.)*

For plurality, approval, score and Borda the count is the totals of the candidates, up to a number added to all of them. For a method that depends only on the pairwise margins it is at most the margins. For BTR-plurality it is the first preferences and the margins. With three candidates these six numbers determine the histogram, and instant runoff has the same count there: each of the six can decide its outcome with the others fixed. Information can be lost in the count, before any strategy: the approval profiles {a, b}, {c} and {a, c}, {b} have the same count. The Equality Criterion is a statement about the count: a ballot and its reverse together have the count of the empty profile (Section 6).

A method A *embeds in* a method B at stage 1 if every ballot of A can be rewritten as a ballot of B, one rewriting for all voters, so that the count of A is a function of the count of B on the rewritten ballots. A voter under B can then say anything a voter under A can, and B still sees what A sees. Plurality embeds in approval (a vote for x as the approval of x alone), approval in score (approval as k, no approval as 0), plurality in instant runoff (a vote for x as a ranking with x first; the plurality count is the count of first preferences), and strict rankings in rankings with equal ranks allowed. Borda embeds in score with k = m − 1: a Borda ballot is a score ballot that uses each score from 0 to m − 1 once. *(Definition; the examples are immediate.)* Stage 1 says what a method could use if voters put into its ballots everything they know. It says nothing about what they will put in.

**Stage 2: what the method carries under strategy.** A voter's *type* is what the best reply depends on: the ranking of the candidates, and the utilities where intensities matter. A *poll* for a method is a shared belief about how the other ballots will fall; in general it is given by the expected shares of the method's ballots, and in the corners only a summary of it matters, such as the leader and the runner-up (*The Common Rationale*, Section 1). At a fixed poll π the best reply sends every type to a ballot, and the *strategic count* of a profile P of types is the count of these ballots,

  E_M^π(P) = τ_M(best replies under M to π, for every voter of P).

Because the count is additive, the strategic count is a linear function of the histogram h(P) of types: E_M^π(P) = M h(P), where the column of the matrix M for a type is the count of that type's single ballot. (Types are grouped into finitely many classes on which the best replies are constant; the size of the electorate is taken as part of every count.)

The map from the profile, unknown to the method, to the strategic count is an *experiment* in the sense of Blackwell (1953): an observation whose distribution depends on an unknown state. Blackwell compares two experiments α and β on the same states in two ways and shows that they agree (cited; his Theorems 3 and 6 for finitely many outcomes, where the converse is due to Sherman and Stein, and Theorem 8 for bounded outcomes):

- α is *more informative* than β if in every decision problem every expected loss that can be attained from observing β can also be attained from observing α;
- α is *sufficient for* β if the observation of β can be produced from that of α by a stochastic transformation that does not depend on the state. β is then also called a *garbling* of α, a later term.

**Matching polls.** To compare two methods at the same poll, their polls have to be matched, since a poll is a belief about the ballots of one method. The matching is part of the comparison. Three are used in the pair notes:

1. *the same summary*: in the corners, the same leader and runner-up for plurality, approval, score and Borda, and the two finalists of a ranked method as the front-runners of plurality;
2. *projection*: where a ballot of B determines a ballot of A (a ranking determines a first preference), the poll of A is the projection of the poll of B; two methods with the same ballots have the same polls;
3. *each at its own*: for a given profile, each method at a poll of its own that the strategic ballots reproduce (a self-confirming poll, Section 3).

The first two ask what the ballots of each method carry given a common belief. The third asks what each carries where its beliefs are correct, and it can give a different answer (Section 3).

Method A *uses no more strategic information* than method B, under a matching, if at all matched polls the strategic count of A is a garbling of that of B. When the experiments are deterministic, as in the corners without ballot mistakes, a garbling is a function: every two profiles that B's strategic count does not tell apart, A's does not either. With ballot mistakes (uncounted marks, *The Common Rationale*, Section 3), random tie-breaking or random electorates the experiments are random, and Blackwell's general form is the one needed. *(Definition; the application of Blackwell's order is ours.)*

**Proposition (counts).** At matched polls, the strategic count of A is a function of the strategic count of B on every profile exactly when every row of M_A is a combination of rows of M_B.

*Proof.* If M_A = K M_B, the count of A is K applied to the count of B. Conversely, let v be a vector of whole numbers with M_B v = 0. It is the difference h − h′ of two histograms, which have the same count under B and therefore under A, so M_A v = 0. The matrices have whole entries, so such v span the kernel of M_B; hence the kernel of M_B lies in that of M_A, which is the statement about rows. ∎ *(Proved here; deterministic experiments.)*

The *rank* of M, not counting the size of the electorate, is then the number of independent numbers about the electorate that the strategic count keeps. It does not depend on a distribution of voters. In the corner (T) it is 1 for plurality, m − 1 for approval and score and m − 2 for Borda; for three candidates it is 2 to 5 for instant runoff and 3 or 5 for BTR-plurality, depending on the poll. *(Computed, `scripts/information/strategic_count.py`.)*

**Lemma (one voter).** Fix matched polls, and suppose that the count of A changes whenever one ballot is replaced by a different one (true of totals and of histograms). If on every profile the strategic count of A is a function of the strategic count of B, then the strategic ballot of A is a function of the strategic ballot of B, voter by voter. Conversely, if the ballot of A is a function of the ballot of B and the count of B is the histogram of ballots, the count of A is a function of the count of B.

*Proof.* Replace a voter by another whose B-ballot is the same: B's count does not change, so A's does not either, so their A-ballots agree. For the converse, A's histogram is the image of B's, and A's count is a function of its histogram. ∎ *(Proved here.)* When the count of B is smaller than the histogram, as for approval and score, a function between the ballots is necessary and not sufficient, and the Proposition decides.

**Bits.** The Lemma gives a measure for the ballots. For a distribution of voters (a culture, for instance impartial culture), the entropy H(β_M) of the strategic ballot β_M at a poll is the information one strategic ballot carries about the voter, the counterpart of the capacity log₂|B| of stage 1. The conditional entropy H(β_A | β_B) is what A's ballot says that B's does not. If it is positive, A's ballot is not a function of B's, and by the Lemma the order fails at that poll. If it is zero, the order holds when the count of B is the histogram, and otherwise has to be checked with the Proposition. The entropies depend on the culture and measure the ballot, not the count: in the corner the strategic Borda ballot carries 1 + log₂ (m − 2)! bits, while the Borda count keeps m − 2 numbers about the electorate, one fewer than the count of score. *(Computed for every pair in the pair notes.)*

Stage 2 does not follow from stage 1. Approval embeds in score, strictly, yet under the common rationale in large electorates the two strategic counts coincide up to the factor k (*Score and Approval*, Section 1): voters do not use the extra capacity. Whether a method can lie below another at stage 1 and above it at stage 2 is open (Section 7).

## 3. The order

**Targets.** A *target* says, for every profile of types, how good each candidate would be as the winner. Two are used: the *majority target*, under which the Condorcet winner is the only acceptable winner where there is one (and nothing is asked otherwise), and the *utilitarian target*, the total utility of the winner.

**Best case.** Blackwell's first form says exactly that the better experiment is at least as good for every target at once. If A uses no more strategic information than B, then any rule for choosing the winner from A's strategic count, judged by any target, can be matched by a rule reading B's strategic count. The best case of Section 1 is taken in this sense: the best use of the information carried by the strategic ballots, not the best outcome over all polls. An outcome that needs a fortunate poll uses information from outside the electorate, the selection among polls described in *Plurality and Approval*, Section 4.

**Worst case.** A poll is *self-confirming* for a method at a profile if the strategic ballots reproduce it (a voting equilibrium in the sense of Myerson and Weber 1993). The worst case of a method at a profile is the worst winner, by the target, that the method's own rule elects at a self-confirming poll.

Self-confirming polls need not exist in a corner: approval has none without a Condorcet winner, Borda has none at all, and instant runoff and BTR-plurality often have none. The corner then says nothing about the worst case at that profile. In a smooth model of beliefs, where the likelihood of every event changes continuously with the poll and the voters' types are spread without atoms, a self-confirming poll should exist for every profile by Brouwer's theorem; this is not proved here (*The Common Rationale*, Section 5). So the worst case is defined in the smooth model, and the corners are where it can be proved. In computations a self-confirming poll is approximated by a poll at which the walk of polls comes to rest, called a *resting poll* in the pair notes.

**The order.** A *lies below* B, for a given target and matching, if A uses no more strategic information than B and, at every profile, B's worst case is at least as good as A's, with one of the two strict. A and B are *strategically equivalent* if each uses no more strategic information than the other and their worst cases agree.

For plurality and approval in the corner (T), with the same leader and runner-up for both: by *Plurality and Approval*, Proposition 1, the strategic plurality count is a function of the strategic approval count at every poll (both front-runners have the same tally under both, and plurality gives the others nothing), and by Proposition 2 every self-confirming approval poll is self-confirming under plurality, with the Condorcet winner as leader. So plurality lies below approval for the majority target, and for the utilitarian target as well whenever there is a Condorcet winner. Score and approval with large electorates are strategically equivalent. *(From the propositions cited.)*

The matching matters for the first half. With each method at its own self-confirming poll, plurality can stand at a poll of two candidates neither of whom is the Condorcet winner, and the pairwise count it then keeps is not in the row of the Condorcet winner that approval keeps. Plurality's strategic count is contained in approval's given the same belief, not across the beliefs that each method can sustain. *(From Propositions 1 and 2 of that note.)*

**Margins.** The order is exact, and it holds in the corners for some pairs. In the smooth model neither half holds exactly for any pair examined so far. The comparison is then reported as two margins (for five methods together in *The Smooth Model across Methods*): for the information, the ranks of the strategic counts and their common part, and the bits that each ballot carries beyond the other; for the worst case, how often over a culture a resting poll elects a winner that the target does not accept. Whether the margins should be turned into an order by a tolerance is left open (Section 7).

## 4. The common rationale

Every voter best-responds to the same poll (a leader and a runner-up) under the same assumption on the likelihood of ties, whatever the method. The rationale, its assumptions and their sources are in [The Common Rationale](rationale.md).

## 5. Results

The results are of three kinds: statements about all methods, which need no model of beliefs beyond the linear regime; one computation for five methods under a single model; and the pairs, compared in their corners.

### 5.1 For all methods: the ladder

*What a strategic ballot can be.* In a large electorate the value of a ballot is linear in what it adds to the count, so a best reply is a vertex of the *ballot polytope*, the convex hull of what single ballots can add ([The Ballot Polytope](ballot-polytope.md), Propositions 1 and 2). A change that adds ballots without adding vertices changes nothing at stage 2: score after approval, cumulative voting after plurality, and equal ranks wherever a ballot with an equal rank is counted as the average of the strict rankings it lies between (averaged Borda scores, every method that reads only the margins, shared first preferences). A change that adds vertices can matter: approval after plurality, whole first preferences for candidates ranked equal at the top, truncated Borda ballots. *(Proved for the linear regime; vertices computed for three and four candidates; checked in the smooth model.)*

*What a count can certify.* Some comparisons need neither a corner nor a computation ([Bounds from Criteria](bounds.md)). A method can tell at a poll whether its leader is the Condorcet winner only if its strategic count contains the leader's row of pairwise margins. That takes 2^{m−1} vertices of the ballot polytope, which plurality and cumulative voting do not have, and a count of rank m − 1. For a method that counts totals this is all the room there is: such a method holds at most the leader's row, approval in its corner holds exactly that, and none that fails to keep opposite voters equal at a poll (the strategic form of the Equality Criterion, Section 6) reaches it there. For methods that count more, such as instant runoff, failing the criterion uses up part of the count on what neither target depends on, without putting the method under a bound. The bounds form a ladder: one comparison (two vertices, rank 1), the leader's row (2^{m−1} vertices, rank m − 1), the whole majority relation (m! vertices, rank m(m − 1)/2). A method on the second level can confirm or reject a leader and cannot name the Condorcet winner, so it depends on the poll for the right leader; only the third level is free of the poll, and there the strategic ballot reveals the whole ranking. Each level also asks something of the voters' beliefs ([What a Ballot Reveals](revelation.md)): a voter chooses from a menu of lotteries, a comparison is revealed only where its race is kept apart from the others, and under certainty about the other ballots no method reveals more than one comparison. The theorem of Gibbard and Satterthwaite excludes a revealing ballot that is the same at every belief. So the top of the ladder is reached only at particular beliefs, such as Borda at the belief in which all ties are equally likely. *(Proved.)*

*The other target.* No ballot reveals the scale of a voter's utilities, under any method ([Intensities and the Utilitarian Target](intensity.md)). A method that counts totals adds up utilities that its ballot polytope has normalised: to a set of approved candidates, a ranking, a single name. Corners of the polytope are what stands between such a count and the sum of utilities with common mean and spread; finer scores do not remove them, and a round set of ballots would. The shape that certifies the leader reveals no intensity, so among counts of totals the majority target and the utilitarian target are a trade-off. *(Proved, large electorates.)*

A level of the ladder therefore asks three things: enough vertices in the ballot polytope, enough rank in the count, and enough uncertainty of the right shape in the voters' beliefs. The first two are properties of the method. The third is a property of the poll.

| What the strategic count certifies | Vertices | Rank | Beliefs | Reached by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One comparison | 2 | 1 | any, certainty included | every method; all that plurality's ballot can carry |
| The leader's row | 2^{m−1} | m − 1 | at least (m − 1)/log₂ m possibilities held open | approval and score in their corner; the most a count of totals can hold |
| The whole majority relation | m! | m(m − 1)/2 | at least log₂ m!/log₂ m possibilities, every pair with a race of its own | only rankings with a count that contains the margins, at particular beliefs |

### 5.2 The two axes

Strategy is one of two axes ([The Honest Axis](honest.md)). On the other, every voter is honest and the target is the utilitarian one; this is the ideal, and its order is the order of stage 1: score voting with a fine scale holds the normalised sum of utilities, and a ranking or an approval ballot is a reduction of it. The two axes are the ends of one line, the sharpness of the voters' beliefs. For plurality, approval, Borda and round ballots the honest ballot is the best reply of a voter who knows nothing about the race, so the honest count is the strategic count at the empty poll. Polls sharpen the beliefs, the empty poll does not confirm itself, and the polls that do are on the side of the majority relation. A method is described by what it holds at each end. Score voting has the widest gap: honest it holds the utilitarian sum, under strategy it is approval. No method leads at both ends. In numbers, for three candidates: honest score voting and honest round ballots give the highest total utility, and the methods with coarser ballots give more total utility at their resting polls than with honest voters, since a strategic ballot brings in intensities that their honest ballot leaves out. *(Proved, with the direction as interpretation; the numbers computed.)*

### 5.3 One model for five methods

The corners are separate assumptions for each method. In one smooth model of beliefs, with three candidates and the same profiles for plurality, approval, Borda, instant runoff and BTR-plurality ([The Smooth Model across Methods](smooth-model.md)), plurality's worst case is far worse than that of the other four under the majority target and under the utilitarian one, and the other four are not ordered: they differ by a few profiles in forty, instant runoff and BTR-plurality resting more often at the Condorcet winner, approval and Borda more often at the utilitarian winner. Approval's resting polls are not all led by the Condorcet winner there, and Borda's polls do come to rest; the sharper statements for these two belong to their corners. *(Computed.)*

**Across the pairs.** For the three pairs that differ in the size of the ballot (plurality against approval and instant runoff, approval against score) the measure gives one picture. In each corner the order is exact: plurality's strategic ballot is a function of approval's and of instant runoff's, and score's and approval's are functions of each other. Away from the corner it breaks smoothly, not at once. For plurality against approval and against instant runoff, what the coarser ballot then keeps that the finer one lacks is information about intensities: which of the approved candidates gets plurality's single vote, whether to desert a favourite in a close race of three. The finer ballot keeps the larger lead (about 1 to 3 bits per voter, against hundredths to tenths of a bit, up to about one bit with five candidates and fully random weights). For score against approval the extra information goes the other way, into the finer ballot (the windows of *Score and Approval*, Section 2), until the electorate is so small that indifference between ballots blurs both. So the order of Section 3 holds in the corners, and away from them it is replaced by a margin that the entropies measure. *(Summary of the computations in the pair notes; interpretation.)* Borda against score differs instead by a constraint on the ballot (each score once), and there the pair is unordered already in the corner from four candidates on: the constraint trades the threshold that score keeps for an order (*Borda and Score*, Section 3). BTR-plurality against instant runoff keeps the ballot and changes only the counting; there the strategic ballots are unordered by a margin of similar size both ways, and the Condorcet criterion of the count does not carry over to the strategic outcome (*Instant Runoff and BTR-Plurality*, Sections 5 to 7).

## 6. Connection with the Equality Criterion

The Equality Criterion (EC-V in *Equality of Voters and Alternatives*) asks that a ballot together with its reverse never changes the outcome. In the terms of Section 2 it is a statement about stage 1: a ballot and its reverse have the count of the empty profile, up to the ties that that note treats. Approval, score and Borda pass, and so does every method whose count is the margins: the pair adds the same number to every total and nothing to any margin. Plurality, as a rule on rankings, instant runoff and BTR-plurality fail (*The Equality Criterion and Reversal*, Section 2, and the tables of *Equality of Voters and Alternatives* for the first two; for BTR-plurality and instant runoff, examples with a cycle in `scripts/information/strategic_count.py`, part 3). For ballots that name a single candidate the criterion cannot be stated, since they have no reverses.

**The strategic form.** Call two voters *opposite* if their utilities are the negatives of each other. A method *keeps opposite voters equal* at a poll if the strategic ballots of two opposite voters together have the same count, whoever the two are. The strategic count then depends on the profile only through the differences between the number of voters of each type and of the opposite type, and on the size of the electorate. *(Definition and immediate consequence, from the linearity of the strategic count.)*

- Approval keeps opposite voters equal at every poll and for any weights of ties: opposite voters cast complementary best ballots (*Plurality and Approval*, Proposition 5, part 3). *(Proved.)*
- Score, Borda and every method that reads only the margins do so as well, at every poll in a large electorate: their ballot polytopes are centrally symmetric, which is what the property amounts to (*The Ballot Polytope*, Proposition 4). *(Proved for the linear regime.)*
- Plurality does so in the corner (T), where the two votes go to the two front-runners. *(Computed for three to six candidates, `strategic_count.py`, part 1.)* This ends with the corner. With ties between a and b and between c and d both likely, a voter with a > b > c > d votes for a and the opposite voter for d, and the pair tilts both races (*computed example*).
- Instant runoff and BTR-plurality, with three candidates, keep opposite voters equal at no poll of their corners. *(Computed, part 2.)*

So the criterion at stage 1 implies its strategic form in large electorates (a ballot and its reverse with the count of nothing make the ballot polytope centrally symmetric), and for the methods examined the two go together, except for plurality inside its corner: the methods that pass see only the differences between opposite voters, and the methods that fail also see how many opposite voters there are. In Proposition 1 of *Plurality and Approval* the extra information of approval is the row of the leader, each entry a pairwise count on which a ballot and its opposite cancel. Whether seeing the opposite pairs is what lets the outcome depend on the choice among self-confirming polls, a choice made from outside the profile, is open. *(Interpretation and question.)*

## 7. Open questions

1. The worst case. Existence of a self-confirming poll for every profile in the smooth model (Brouwer), and what replaces it in a corner without one: the cycles of the walk or the endpoints of the walk with memory. Should the margins of Section 3 be turned into an order by a tolerance, and how would a tolerance in the information combine with one in the worst case?
2. The matching. Under the same belief plurality's strategic count is contained in approval's; with each method at its own self-confirming poll it is not (Section 3). Which of the two should the order rest on, and is there a pair that is ordered under both?
3. The further pairs of Section 5: approval and instant runoff, the supplementary vote and the other differences that need four candidates, whole first preferences for equal tops; and whether a method can lie below another at stage 1 and above it at stage 2.
4. A measure of the count that depends on a culture as the bits of the ballot do, for instance the information that the strategic count of a large electorate carries about the shares of the types.
5. Measure the gap in the Ballot Front Finder Lab: the share of profiles in which the strategic outcome depends on the starting poll, per method.
6. The link with the Equality Criterion (Section 6, and *Bounds from Criteria*, Section 4, where it is a ceiling for methods that count totals): do the methods that see the pairs of opposite voters owe their additional self-confirming polls to them? And the strategic form for more than three candidates under the ranked methods.
7. A criterion in the spirit of the Smith set: a method reaches the Smith set under the common rationale within N rounds from every poll. Approval with the leader rule fails it (*Plurality and Approval*, Section 5), rarely; with memory of who has led it holds with N = 2(m − 1) (Section 6 there). Which other methods satisfy it, and is 2(m − 1) the right bound for a rule that does not stop?
