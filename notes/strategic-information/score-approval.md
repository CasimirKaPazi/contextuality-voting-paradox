# Score and Approval under a Common Rationale

*Working note, part of [Strategic Information](README.md). It compares score voting with scores 0 to k with approval voting under the common rationale of [The Common Rationale](rationale.md). Conventions as in the README. The checks are in `scripts/information/score_exact.py`, `score_window.py` and `stage2_bits.py`.*

## 1. The reduction to approval

Under the same rationale, score voting with scores 0 to k reduces to approval with every ballot multiplied by k (proved here: the tie between a₁ and a₂ forces the scores of the two to k and 0, and each tie with a₁ forces every other score to k or 0, according to the sign of u_x − u_{a₁}). Tallies are scaled by k, so outcomes and self-confirming polls coincide with those of approval. This is the case of (T) in the equivalence of Núñez and Laslier (2014): additive rules with the same extremal ballots are strategically equivalent in large elections, and the equivalence fails for small electorates (cited). Under this rationale score voting is exactly as good as approval and never worse, which is the first half of the claim that score voting dominates approval. The reduction does not need (T). For any weights of ties, as in *The Common Rationale*, Section 2, the value of a score ballot is linear in the scores, Σ s(x) c(x), so a best ballot gives each candidate k or 0 according to the sign of its coefficient, and the best score ballot is k times the best approval ballot whenever no coefficient vanishes. *(Proved, from *Plurality and Approval*, Proposition 5.)* An intermediate score can only be a best reply through what the linear valuation leaves out: near ties in which one candidate is a few points ahead, and the voter's own points as a share of a small electorate. In Laslier's binomial model with the others giving 0 or k points, exact best replies use an intermediate score as the unique best ballot for 5 or 6 of 50 random voters at 10 voters, for at most 1 of 50 from 20 voters on, and never from 40 voters on except once in 50 (with k = 2; three and four candidates; `scripts/information/score_exact.py`). With k = 3 no unique best ballot used an intermediate score: when every other voter gives 0 or k, the scores 1 to k − 1 all win a tie for their candidate without creating a new one, so they are interchangeable. *(Computed.)* So score voting carries no information beyond approval under the common rationale, except through the arithmetic of very small electorates.

## 2. When intermediate scores help

Warren Smith (2005) gives the conditions under which extreme scores are optimal and examples in which they are not. His Theorem 3 assumes that near ties of two candidates are rare, near ties of three negligible, and that the chance of breaking a tie between A and B is proportional to the voter's score difference for A and B (cited); this last condition is exactly the linear valuation above, and his Theorem 2 (candidates ordered by chance of election, each far more likely than the next) is a form of (T). With full knowledge of the other ballots, an approval-style ballot is again optimal (his Theorem 4, cited). His counterexamples sit between the two: the voter knows the others' totals to within less than one ballot, but not exactly. In his Section 3.1 the others' totals are (1, 1.1, 1) or (0, 1.1, 1.2) for A, B, C with equal chance, the voter's utilities are 10, 5, 0, and the scores range over [0, 1]. B needs more than 0.1 to pass C in the second state and less than 0.9 to stay behind A in the first, so every ballot (1, s, 0) with s in that window wins A and B once each, for expected utility 7.5, while the best approval-style ballot gets 5. *(Checked exactly, `scripts/information/score_window.py`; his Sections 3.2 and 3.3 give smooth versions with three and four voters, the best score for B near 0.58 and 0.46, and the script reproduces 3.2 by simulation.)* The mechanism is a window: two pivotal events for the same candidate need different amounts of points, and a score between them wins one without losing the other. A window needs the voter's ballot to be large compared with the spread of the others' tallies, so it belongs to small electorates or sharp knowledge; Smith calls his assumptions plausible in large elections and usually false in small ones, and reports that in his simulations the gain from such strategy over honest threshold voting is of order 1 in 1000 (cited). When the other voters also use the whole scale (0 to 4) the windows are not killed by the lattice: in Laslier's binomial model the unique best ballot used an intermediate score for 6 of 40 random voters at 10 voters, 1 to 3 of 40 at 30 voters, and none at 90, with each mark uncounted with probability 1/20 or 1/200 (*computed*, same script). So the reduction to approval fails exactly where the linear valuation fails, and the linear valuation is the large-electorate form of the common rationale.

## 3. Stage 2 in bits

In large electorates the best score ballot is k times the best approval ballot for any weights of ties, except for valuations of measure zero (Section 1). So each strategic ballot is a function of the other, H(β_score | β_appr) = H(β_appr | β_score) = 0, the strategic score count is k times the strategic approval count, and the two methods are strategically equivalent at stage 2 ([README](README.md), Section 2), although the capacity of score at stage 1 is (k + 1)^m ballots against 2^m. *(Proved, from Section 1.)*

In small electorates (Laslier's model, the others voting 0 or k, shares 0.55, 0.45, 0.35, three candidates), with 20 or 40 other voters the approval ballot is a function of the score ballot (H(β_appr | β_score) = 0) and the score ballot says 0.03 to 0.08 bits more, from the one to four voters in 300 whose best score ballot is not k times their best approval ballot: here the score ballot lies above the approval ballot, by a small margin. (This compares ballots; whether the approval totals are then a function of the score totals is not checked.) With 10 other voters neither ballot is a function of the other (H(β_score | β_appr) is about 0.45 to 0.48 bits, H(β_appr | β_score) 0.02 to 0.12); many voters are then indifferent between several ballots, and part of these figures is the rule that breaks such ties towards the sincere ballot. *(Computed, `scripts/information/stage2_bits.py`.)*

## 4. Open question

Score against approval in small electorates, where intermediate scores can be best replies through windows between pivotal events (Section 2, Smith 2005): do they carry information that the tally uses, and does it improve the outcome? A window is information about the others' ballots finer than one ballot, so the question is also whether a poll can ever carry that much.
