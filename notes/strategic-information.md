# Strategic Information: Plurality and Approval under a Common Rationale

*Working note, first draft. It proposes a way to compare voting methods by how much of the electorate's information they can use when every voter follows the same strategic rationale, and carries the comparison out for plurality and approval voting, with score voting as a first extension. Statements are marked as cited, proved here, computed on random profiles, or interpretation. References marked † are cited from memory and have not been checked against the original. The checks are in `scripts/information/strategic_information.py`, `ties.py`, `ballot_mistakes.py`, `lost_marks_walk.py` and `score_exact.py`; Propositions 1 to 3, the statement on the Smith set and the steps of Proposition 4 are checked in Lean in `lean/LeaderRule.lean`, with the best replies as definitions; Propositions 5 and 6 and the counting of Proposition 4 in `lean/StrategicInformation.lean`, and Proposition 4 as one statement with the passage from positions to utilities in `lean/MemoryWalk.lean`.*

## 1. The question

The impossibility theorems of Arrow and Gibbard describe the upper end of the space of voting methods: once a method is good enough, its remaining defects are trade-offs, and no method has all the properties at once. They say nothing about methods that lie below that frontier. A method can be worse than another without any trade-off, if the other does at least as well in every respect that both are judged by. The aim of this note is to make such comparisons precise for methods that are similar enough to be compared pair by pair.

The comparison has two steps. The first asks for the best use a method can make of the information in the electorate under ideal assumptions about the voters. The second asks for the worst outcome under the same assumptions. If one method is at least as good as another in both, and better in one, the other lies below the frontier. Neither step compares sincere ballots: voters who leave gains on the table are not the point. Both methods are played by voters who follow the same rationale, and the rationale must be stated without reference to the method.

## 2. The common rationale

A *poll* is a shared belief about the race, given by a leader a₁ and a runner-up a₂ (in Myerson and Weber 1993, the voters' perceptions of which pairs of candidates are in contention). A voter values a ballot by its effect on pivotal events, in the manner of Myerson and Weber (1993): if s(x) is what the ballot adds to the tally of candidate x and u the voter's utility, a tie between x and y gives the ballot the value (s(x) − s(y))(u_x − u_y). The setting is that of Myerson and Weber's voting equilibria, applied here to the comparison of methods (the *common rationale*: every voter best-responds to the same poll under the same assumption on ties, whatever the method). One assumption on the likelihood of ties is used, in the sense that one tie is so much more likely than another that the second matters only when the first leaves the choice open (a voter then maximises lexicographically, event by event):

- **(T)** for every candidate x other than a₁, the most likely tie involving x is the tie with a₁; the most likely tie involving a₁ is the tie with a₂; ties of three or more candidates are less likely than the ties of two among them.

This is in substance the ordering condition on pivot probabilities under which Myerson and Weber (1993) derive their predictions, as described by Durand, Macé and Núñez (2019). No comparison is made between ties that share no candidate, for instance between a₁ against a₄ and a₂ against a₃. Laslier (2006, Lemma 2) states an order of this kind for large electorates and uses it for the leader rule (his Theorem 1): types of voters replicated n times, scores without ties, and each approval left unrecorded with a small probability ε < 1/v, independently across voters and candidates, where v is the size of the unreplicated electorate. The lemma orders all ties involving a given candidate by the ranks of the others. Its proof covers the ties of a candidate with those ranked below it, which gives the part of (T) concerning the two front-runners. It does not cover the ties of a candidate ranked third or lower with the candidates above it, and in his model that part fails: those ties stay of the same order however large the electorate (Section 4e). Here (T) is therefore an assumption, not a consequence of the model of ballot mistakes, and Section 4e describes what replaces it there. The abstract of the published version (Laslier 2009) states the order of ties as a supposition: "It is supposed that voters evaluate the relative likelihood of pairwise ties" from statistical information about the scores, and the Condorcet winner is elected "at equilibrium". Read this way, the published result is Proposition 2 below with (T) as an assumption about voters' beliefs; the paper itself has not been checked. The assumption is the same for every method, and it is the rationale used throughout.

Preferences are strict. With this rationale the best replies are as follows (proved here; checked by brute force over all ballots in the script):

- **Plurality.** Vote for whichever of a₁ and a₂ you prefer. The tie between them decides alone, since a vote for anyone else has no effect on it.
- **Approval.** Approve a₁ exactly when you prefer a₁ to a₂, and approve every other candidate exactly when you prefer that candidate to a₁. The first tie fixes the ballot on a₁ and a₂ (one of the two approved, not both, since approving both abstains from their comparison). The ties with a₁ then fix every other candidate. This is the leader rule (related to the k-pragmatists of Reijngoud and Endriss 2012, voters who use only the top k of a poll), Laslier's "lexicographic response" (2006, Theorem 1, cited; there derived from his Lemma 2, so in his model it holds for the front-runners but not for candidates ranked third or lower, Section 4e). In particular a₂ is approved exactly when it is preferred to a₁. Leader-rule ballots are sincere, and admissible in the sense of Brams and Fishburn (1983): the favourite is always approved and the least preferred candidate never (Laslier 2006, Corollary 1 and Section 1, cited; checked).

## 2a. When the poll is tied

(T) needs a poll without ties, as does Laslier's lemma. When two contenders are tied in the poll, a voter cannot know which of two races is pivotal. Suppose the two races are of the same order of likelihood, with some ratio of their probabilities, and every other race involving the candidates concerned is less likely; this is not covered by Laslier's lemma and is assumed here.

- **A tie for second** between x₂ and x₃. The races of a₁ against x₂ and against x₃ weigh together. A voter who prefers a₁ to both approves it, one who prefers both to a₁ does not, and a voter who ranks a₁ between them approves it exactly when the utility of a₁ exceeds the weighted mean of the utilities of x₂ and x₃. Every other candidate is decided against a₁ as before.
- **A tie for first** between a₁ and a₂. The voter approves the preferred of the two and not the other. Every other candidate x is weighed against both: approved if preferred to both, not if both are preferred to it, and otherwise approved exactly when its utility exceeds the weighted mean of those of a₁ and a₂.

In both cases the ballot stays sincere, and intensities of preference enter only for candidates ranked between the tied contenders. *(Proved here under the stated assumption.)* Laslier observes that without a Condorcet winner every equilibrium score vector has a tie between the two first candidates or among the second-ranked ones (2006, after Corollary 2, cited). Intensities therefore enter exactly where the majority relation cycles: where ordinal information runs out, the rational ballot draws on cardinal information. *(Interpretation.)*

## 3. What the ballots carry

Write N(x > y) for the number of voters who prefer x to y.

**Proposition 1.** Under the common rationale, for a poll with leader a₁ and runner-up a₂:

1. a₁ has the tally N(a₁ > a₂) and a₂ the tally N(a₂ > a₁), under plurality and under approval alike;
2. every plurality ballot lies inside the approval ballot of the same voter, and the plurality ballot is a function of the approval ballot: a₁ if a₁ is approved, a₂ otherwise;
3. every other candidate x has the tally N(x > a₁) under approval, and none under plurality.

*Proof.* A voter votes for a₁ under plurality exactly when they prefer a₁ to a₂, which is exactly when they approve a₁; the same holds for a₂. This gives 1 and 2. Under approval, x outside the pair is approved exactly by the voters who prefer x to a₁, which gives 3. ∎

The approval tallies in 1 and 3 are Laslier's Theorem 2 (2006, cited), stated there for equilibrium score vectors and proved from the best replies alone, so that it holds for any poll. The comparison with plurality is the addition here. Parts 1 and 2 use (T) only for the front-runners, so they also hold in Laslier's model of ballot mistakes; part 3 uses the rest of (T) (Section 4e).

*Reading.* In the matrix of pairwise counts, strategic plurality reveals one entry, the comparison of the two front-runners. Strategic approval reveals that entry and the whole row of the leader: how every candidate fares against a₁. In the language of Blackwell (1953)†, the plurality ballot is a garbling of the approval ballot, so any use that can be made of the strategic plurality ballots can also be made of the strategic approval ballots, and not conversely. This is the first step of Section 1 for this pair, for a fixed poll. *(Proved; the interpretation as a Blackwell comparison is ours.)*

## 4. Which polls confirm themselves

The poll is not given from outside. A poll is *self-confirming* if the tally it produces ranks a₁ first and a₂ second, strictly (a voting equilibrium in the sense of Myerson and Weber, an outcome that justifies the perceptions it came from; Laslier's equilibrium score vector).

**Proposition 2.** Under the common rationale:

1. Under plurality, a poll (a₁, a₂) is self-confirming exactly when a₁ beats a₂ by majority and some voter prefers a₂ to a₁.
2. Under approval, a poll (a₁, a₂) is self-confirming exactly when a₁ is the Condorcet winner and a₂ is its strongest challenger, the unique candidate x maximising N(x > a₁) (Laslier's *best contender*).
3. Every self-confirming approval poll is a self-confirming plurality poll.

Statement 2 is Laslier's Corollary 2 (2006, cited): an equilibrium without ties elects a Condorcet winner, and a Condorcet winner with a unique best contender gives a unique equilibrium. Statements 1 and 3 are the addition here. Statement 2 needs all of (T); in Laslier's model of ballot mistakes it fails (Section 4e). Statement 3 uses only the tallies of the front-runners and holds there too.

*Proof.* Under plurality the tally is N(a₁ > a₂) for a₁, N(a₂ > a₁) for a₂ and zero for the rest, which gives 1. Under approval the poll confirms itself exactly when N(a₁ > a₂) > N(a₂ > a₁) > N(x > a₁) for every other x. The first inequality says a₁ beats a₂. With it, N(a₂ > a₁) < n/2, so the second says that every other x also loses to a₁, and that a₂ is the unique strongest challenger. Conversely, if a₁ is the Condorcet winner and a₂ its unique strongest challenger, then N(a₁ > a₂) > n/2 > N(a₂ > a₁) > N(x > a₁). Statement 3 follows, since N(a₂ > a₁) > N(x > a₁) ≥ 0. ∎

The proposition is checked on random profiles (impartial culture and a two-dimensional spatial model, 101 voters, three to six candidates), with profiles skipped where a tie in the tally decides.

*Consequences.*

- When there is a Condorcet winner, approval has exactly one self-confirming poll and elects the Condorcet winner. Plurality has one self-confirming poll for every pair of candidates (with the majority winner of the pair as leader), so it can elect any candidate that beats at least one other. This is Laslier's result on the approval side, under (T); it agrees with Myerson and Weber's observation that plurality restricts the winner's position little while approval leads to the median (cited). In a setting with common values, Goertz and Maniquet (2011) find approval the only simple scoring rule with at least one equilibrium that aggregates information efficiently (cited).
- When there is no Condorcet winner, approval has no self-confirming poll at all, and plurality still has many. In approval the absence of a fixed point is located exactly on the far side of the walls of *Walls and Strategy*, Section 2: the strategic regress becomes visible as a poll that cannot settle. Plurality settles in any case, because it reads only one comparison and cannot see the cycle. *(Interpretation.)*
- Under plurality, which self-confirming poll occurs is not determined by the electorate: the ballots carry no information that selects among them. The selection comes from outside the profile (history, media, the order in which candidates entered), the placement "in the rule" of *Where the Symmetry Goes* in a strategic form. Under approval, when a Condorcet winner exists, the profile determines the poll. *(Interpretation.)*

## 4a. The majority switch

Proposition 2 concerns polls that confirm themselves, and it says something only when a Condorcet winner exists. A weaker property holds for every profile: approval can leave a leader whom a majority rejects, where plurality stays. Laslier remarks that without a Condorcet winner any announced winner is defeated under approval by a candidate preferred to it by more than half the population (2006, after Corollary 2, cited). Proposition 3 makes this precise: the defeat can take two rounds, since a leader who beats its runner-up keeps the lead for one round, with a new runner-up. Let the poll be replaced each round by the two highest tallies of the round before (the *walk*; a political tâtonnement in the sense of Durand, Macé and Núñez 2019, or iterative voting with poll information as in Meir et al. 2010 and Reijngoud and Endriss 2012).

**Proposition 3.** Under the common rationale, with approval:

1. whenever the leader changes from one round to the next, the new leader beats the old one by majority;
2. a leader beaten by majority by some candidate does not lead two rounds in a row;
3. a Condorcet winner, once it leads, keeps the lead.

*Proof.* Let the poll be (a₁, a₂). For 1, the new leader L has a higher tally than a₁ and a₂. If L = a₂, then N(a₂ > a₁) > N(a₁ > a₂). Otherwise N(L > a₁) exceeds both N(a₁ > a₂) and N(a₂ > a₁), one of which is at least n/2. For 2, suppose a₁ is beaten and still leads the next tally. Let c be its strongest challenger, the x maximising N(x > a₁); then N(c > a₁) > n/2 and c has the highest tally after a₁, so the next poll is (a₁, c). Its tally gives a₁ the count N(a₁ > c) < n/2 and c the count N(c > a₁) > n/2, so a₁ loses the lead. Statement 3 is the converse half of Proposition 2: as leader, a Condorcet winner gets N(a₁ > a₂) > n/2 and every other candidate a count below n/2. ∎

Starting from the sincere plurality count, the plurality winner p is therefore replaced within two rounds whenever some candidate beats it by majority, and the first new leader is such a candidate. Under plurality, by Proposition 2, every poll whose leader beats its runner-up stays where it is. This is the ability to make the switch by majority, and it needs no Condorcet winner. *(Proved; ties in tallies excluded; checked on random profiles.)*

The sequence of leaders is a walk along majority defeats: by the proof of parts 1 and 2, each new leader is the strongest challenger of the one before. It stops exactly at a Condorcet winner. Without one it cannot stop.

**The Smith set.** The Smith set (also called GETCHA, or the top cycle) is the smallest set of candidates each of whom beats every candidate outside it; a Condorcet winner is the case of one candidate. By part 1, once the leader is in the Smith set it stays there, since no candidate outside the set beats a member. Without a Condorcet winner the walk therefore cycles inside the Smith set once it has entered it, and the cycle takes the place of the self-confirming poll. *(Proved.)*

Entering is not guaranteed. The walk follows the strongest defeat of the current leader, and a cycle outside the Smith set can have stronger defeats than those inflicted by the Smith set. An example with 102 voters and candidates a, b, c, d: three groups of 35, 34 and 33 voters hold the cyclic rankings a > b > d, b > d > a and d > a > b, and in each group all but four voters put c second, the four others last. Then c beats every other candidate by 59 to 61 votes and is the Condorcet winner, with no first preferences, while the cycle a, b, d has margins of 67 to 69. From the plurality count the leader runs a, d, b, a, … forever, with c as runner-up from the second round on, and every tally on the way is strict. *(Computed; the profile is checked in the script.)* Among random profiles such walks are rare: of 24,800 walks from every starting poll (four to six candidates, both models), 43 ended in a cycle outside the Smith set, some of them through ties between challengers; from the plurality count, 1,197 of 1,200 profiles reached the Smith set. Brams and Fishburn (1983, example 7, p. 120) already give an example in which adjustment to polls cycles although a Condorcet winner exists (cited via Laslier 2006). *(Computed, no claim:* from every starting poll, the walk ends at the Condorcet winner in all spatial profiles and in 98 to 100 percent of the starting polls under impartial culture; among the profiles where the plurality winner is beaten, the leader after two rounds beats it in 177 of 178 spatial profiles and in 86 percent under impartial culture, after twenty rounds in 177 of 178 and in 67 percent; the difference is the walk moving on within a cycle.)

The same walk can be read in two ways. As a model of strategic voters under approval, its rounds are successive polls. As a counting method on ranked ballots, its rounds are computed from the ballots themselves, starting from the plurality count, and Proposition 3 then describes the count. Such a method obtains the outcome that the poll dynamics would reach without depending on voters to carry the dynamics out. It is implemented as Leader-AV-r (r counting rounds) in the Ballot Front Finder Lab. *(Interpretation.)*

## 4b. Memory: reaching the Smith set

The walk of Section 4a fails to enter the Smith set because it always moves to the strongest defeat of the current leader, and a cycle outside the Smith set can hold all the strongest defeats. One change of the poll rule removes this: a candidate who has led once does not lead again.

The memory is not an assumption about voters. Voters still cast leader-rule ballots against whatever poll they are shown, and nothing in their rationale would make them exclude a former leader. The rule belongs to whoever produces the poll, or, read as a counting method, to the method itself. Its role here is to separate the two walks under the criterion of reaching the Smith set: the walk of Section 4a fails it, the walk with memory meets it.

**The walk with memory.** At each round the poll is (L, R). A candidate is *eligible* if it has never led, or is the current leader L; R is always eligible. Voters cast leader-rule ballots against (L, R). The next leader is the eligible candidate with the highest tally, and the next runner-up the eligible candidate with the highest tally after it. The walk stops when no eligible candidate other than the leader is left.

**Proposition 4.** Let the walk with memory start from any poll with an eligible runner-up, and suppose that no two eligible candidates tie in a tally. Then, with m candidates:

1. whenever the leader changes, the new leader has never led before and beats the old leader by majority;
2. a leader beaten by an eligible candidate loses the lead within two rounds;
3. a leader who beats every eligible candidate keeps the lead for good;
4. the leader changes at most m − 1 times, and not after round 2(m − 1);
5. the final leader is in the Smith set and beats every candidate that never led.

*Proof.* Parts 1 to 3 are the arguments of Proposition 3 with all candidates replaced by the eligible ones: the old leader's tally is N(L > R) and the runner-up's is N(R > L), both computed against an eligible runner-up, so a candidate who overtakes both beats L, and if L keeps the lead while beaten by an eligible candidate, its strongest eligible challenger becomes runner-up and wins the next tally. For 3, the eligible set does not change while the leader stays, so N(L > R) > n/2 > N(x > L) for every eligible x holds in every later round. For 4, every new leader is new, so there are at most m − 1 changes; after each change the next one comes within two rounds by 2 or never by 3, and the same holds for the first. For 5, once the leader is in the Smith set it stays there by 1. If the final leader were outside, no member of the Smith set would ever have led, so all of them would be eligible and would beat it, and by 2 it would not be final. If the walk stops because every candidate has led, members of the Smith set have led, so the final leader is in it. It beats every candidate that never led, since by 2 an eligible candidate beating it would take the lead. ∎

*(Proved; steps 1 to 3 and the step from a dominant set to an eligible challenger are checked in Lean, and so are parts 4 and 5 for the walk given by its properties, with no pairwise ties between candidates; checked on random profiles, where the bound 2(m − 1) is attained.)* On the profile of Section 4a the walk with memory runs a, d, b, c and stays at c, the Condorcet winner.

The walk with memory satisfies the criterion asked for in Section 9, question 7, with N = 2(m − 1). It does so by using information that a single tally does not hold, the record of who has led. Without that record the information of Proposition 1, the whole row of the leader, is exactly what leads the walk to the strongest defeat. With it, the row is used to choose among candidates not yet tried. *(Interpretation.)*

As an explicit counting method on ranked ballots, started from the plurality count, the walk with memory is a Smith-efficient method of the agenda type: it follows defeats through the candidates, each tried at most once as leader, and stops at the last leader, who beats every candidate not yet tried. For sequential pairwise procedures with an agenda, ending in the top cycle is classical (Miller 1977†). The winner within the Smith set depends on the start and on the order of the walk, as with an agenda chosen by the tallies. The method is a consequence of the analysis, not a model of how voters or polls behave.

## 4c. Away from the corner

Assumption (T) is a limit: each candidate's most likely tie is infinitely more likely than its others. The valuation of Section 2 is defined for any weights. Let p(x, y) = p(y, x) ≥ 0 be the weights of the ties, shared by all voters as part of the poll. A ballot that adds s(x) to the tally of x has the value Σ p(x, y)(s(x) − s(y))(u_x − u_y) over pairs, and this value is linear in s, with the coefficient

  c(x) = Σ_y p(x, y)(u_x − u_y) = w(x)(u_x − E_{π_x} u)

for candidate x, where w(x) = Σ_y p(x, y) and π_x is the lottery that gives each other candidate y the probability p(x, y)/w(x). Call π_x the *virtual opponent* of x.

**Proposition 5 (any weights).** For every system of weights:

1. the best approval ballot approves x exactly when the voter prefers x to its virtual opponent, u_x > E_{π_x} u, so the approval tally of x is the number of voters who prefer x to π_x;
2. the best plurality vote goes to a candidate maximising c(x), and that candidate is approved in the best approval ballot;
3. a voter and the opposite voter (utility −u, the same poll) cast complementary best approval ballots, so together they add one approval to every candidate.

*Proof.* 1 is the sign of the coefficient c(x). For 2, the coefficients sum to zero, since each pair contributes p(x, y)(u_x − u_y) + p(y, x)(u_y − u_x) = 0, so the largest is positive (generically) and the candidate is approved. For 3, replacing u by −u replaces every c(x) by −c(x). ∎

In the corner (T), π_x is the leader for every x ≠ a₁ and the runner-up for a₁, and part 1 is Proposition 1: the tally N(x > a₁) is the special case of a count against a lottery. For any weights the favourite is approved and the least preferred candidate is not, since their coefficients are positive and negative; with three candidates the ballot is therefore sincere. Beyond that, away from the corner the approval ballot need not be sincere: with random weights on three to five candidates, about 4 percent of best ballots approve a candidate while leaving out one the voter prefers (*computed*). The inclusion in part 2 holds everywhere. That the plurality ballot is a function of the approval ballot holds only in the corner; in general the plurality vote is the approved candidate with the largest coefficient, which the approval ballot does not show.

**Proposition 6 (a finite corner, that is, weights close enough to (T) that the best replies do not change).** Let g be the smallest difference between the utilities of two candidates for any voter, and D the largest range of a voter's utilities. Suppose that for some δ with 2δD < g:

- Σ_{y ≠ a₁, a₂} p(a₁, y) ≤ δ p(a₁, a₂), and the same for a₂;
- for every other candidate x: Σ_{y ≠ a₁} p(x, y) ≤ δ p(x, a₁);
- for every other candidate x: Σ_y p(x, y) ≤ δ p(a₁, a₂).

Then the best approval ballot is the leader-rule ballot and the best plurality vote goes to the preferred of a₁ and a₂, for every voter. The last condition is needed only for plurality.

*Proof.* For x ≠ a₁, c(x) = p(x, a₁)(u_x − u_{a₁}) plus terms whose total size is at most δ p(x, a₁) D < p(x, a₁) g, so its sign is that of u_x − u_{a₁}; the same argument with a₂ in place of a₁ decides a₁. For plurality, the preferred front-runner has a coefficient of at least p(a₁, a₂)(g − δD), the other front-runner a negative one, and every other candidate at most δ p(a₁, a₂) D, which is smaller since 2δD < g. ∎

*(Proved; checked in Lean, and on 3000 random systems of weights within the bound.)* So Propositions 1 to 4 do not need the limit, only that at every poll involved each candidate's main tie outweighs its others by a factor above 2D/g. The factor is set by the finest distinction of preference in the electorate. Applied voter by voter, the same proof bounds the damage when the condition fails for some voters: the ballots differ from the leader rule only for voters whose smallest utility difference is at most 2δ times their range, so each tally moves by at most their number, and every strict inequality between tallies in Propositions 1 to 4 with a margin above twice that number survives. The conclusions degrade continuously as the weights leave the corner. *(Proved.)*

What is not yet available away from the corner is the walk: Propositions 2 to 4 also use how the poll, and with it the weights, follows from the tally of the previous round. In the corner this is the ranking of the tallies; in general it needs a model of how tie probabilities depend on the tallies (binomial errors as in Laslier, or Poisson populations).

## 4d. Ties

The propositions exclude ties. Three kinds can occur: ties between pairwise counts, N(x > y) = N(y > x), possible only with an even number of voters; ties between tallies in a poll, for instance two challengers with the same count against the leader; and indifferences in a voter's preferences, which Section 2a touches. The first two are settled here.

**Proposition 7.** Under the common rationale, with approval and the walks of Sections 4a and 4b:

1. If no two candidates tie pairwise, ties between tallies do no harm: with any rule for breaking them, Propositions 2 to 4 hold as stated.
2. With pairwise ties, every change of leader goes to a candidate that beats or ties the old leader. A leader in the Smith set (each member beats every non-member) keeps the walk in it.
3. With pairwise ties and the walk with memory, if ties between tallies are broken by a rule that depends only on the tallies and on who is eligible, the walk settles by round 2(m − 1) in the Smith set. With any other rule, random tie-breaking included, it still settles in the Smith set, since there are at most m − 1 changes, but not necessarily by that round; with random tie-breaking the final leader beats or ties every candidate that never led, with probability one.

*Proof.* Every tally is a pairwise count: N(L > R) for the leader and N(x > L) for every other candidate. For 1: a candidate x reaching the top beside or above the leader has N(x > L) at least max(N(L > R), N(R > L)), which is at least n/2, and with no pairwise ties more than n/2, so x beats L; a beaten leader that keeps the lead hands the runner-up position to a strongest challenger, any of which beats it, and loses the next tally strictly; a Condorcet winner as leader has a tally above n/2 and every other candidate one below, so no tie arises. The arguments of Propositions 2 to 4 go through with these inequalities. For 2, the same computation gives N(x > L) ≥ n/2, so x beats or ties L, and a member of the Smith set is not beaten or tied by a non-member. For 3: changes bring new leaders, so there are at most m − 1. When the leader is kept, the challengers' tallies against it do not change and neither does the set of eligible candidates, so a rule depending only on these chooses the same runner-up again and the poll repeats; a leader kept for two rounds is kept for good, and the counting of Proposition 4 applies. A final leader outside the Smith set would face eligible members who beat it strictly, so it would lose the next tally; this uses only that the runner-up has a largest eligible tally, whatever the rule for ties. With random tie-breaking a tie repeated every round is eventually lost, so with probability one the walk stops only at a leader that no eligible candidate beats or ties, or when every candidate has led. ∎

*(Proved; checked on random profiles with odd and even electorates and both tie-breaking rules, `scripts/information/ties.py`. In even electorates a fifth to over a third of the changes go along ties.)* The assumption of no ties in Sections 4a and 4b can therefore be dropped: it fixes only the strictness of the defeats along the walk and, with random tie-breaking, the bound on the number of rounds. Part 3 is checked in Lean without any assumption on pairwise ties: with deterministic tie-breaking and the bound (`memory_walk_ties` in `MemoryWalk.lean`), and with any tie-breaking without the bound (`memory_walk_settles`); the statement with probability one is not.

**The Schwartz set.** The Schwartz set (also called GOCHA; Schwartz 1986†) is the union of the smallest sets that no outside candidate beats; it lies inside the Smith set and is smaller when pairwise ties occur. The walks do not end in it in general, because they move along ties, and a member of a Schwartz component can be tied with an outsider. Among random profiles with even electorates in which the two sets differ, the walk with memory ended in the Smith set every time and outside the Schwartz set in 18 per cent of the profiles with a fixed tie-break and 53 per cent with a random one (*computed*, `scripts/information/ties.py`). The Schwartz set appears with a different rule for ties: if a tie for the lead never dethrones the current leader, every change is a strict defeat (the new leader's tally exceeds both N(L > R) and, weakly, N(R > L)), so no set that no outsider beats is ever left once the leader is in it. *(Proved; checked in Lean, `incumbent_change_strict`, `incumbent_stays`.)* It never left the Schwartz set once inside, and ended in it in 1414 of 1415 profiles where the two sets differ without memory and in 1404 of 1407 with memory; with memory the exceptions are walks stopped at a leader that the remaining candidates only tie (*computed*). Entering the Schwartz set is not guaranteed in general, for the same reason as for the Smith set in Section 4a.

## 4e. Ballot mistakes: Laslier's model at finite electorates

Assumption (T) is meant to come from ballot mistakes: a voter's ballot matters only if the race is close, and mistakes in recording make some close races much more likely than others. Laslier's model makes this concrete: voter types replicated n times, each mark recorded with probability 1 − ε independently, the winner drawn among the highest totals. A mark that fails to be recorded is simply not counted (an *uncounted mark*; Laslier's "trembling ballots"); no mark is ever added that the voter did not make, so a counted tally can fall below the intended one but never rise above it. In this model the best replies can be computed exactly for finite electorates, without the limit and without the linear valuation of Section 2 (`scripts/information/ballot_mistakes.py`, with ε = 1/20 and exact integer arithmetic). *(Computed throughout.)*

**The front-runners.** The ties of the leader and of the runner-up with the other candidates become negligible exponentially fast against the tie between the two: for vote shares 0.55, 0.45, 0.35 and 0.20 the ratio of the leader's tie with the third candidate to its tie with the runner-up is about 10⁻¹¹ at 80 voters and 10⁻⁴⁰ at 320. Accordingly the best plurality vote is the preferred front-runner already in moderate electorates, even in a close race of three, and so is the part of the approval ballot that concerns the two front-runners.

**The other candidates.** A candidate x ranked third or lower can only be pivotal if every candidate above it falls to its level, since a mark can go uncounted but none is ever added. Its ties with the different candidates above it then require the same losses and are of the same order. Their ratios converge to positive constants close to ((s_y − s_x)/(s_{a₁} − s_x))², with s the vote shares: for the shares above, the third candidate's tie with the runner-up weighs about 0.23 of its tie with the leader, and the fourth candidate's ties with the runner-up and with the third weigh about 0.50 and 0.18. The second half of Laslier's Lemma 2, which makes the tie with the leader infinitely more likely, does not hold, and his proof does not cover it. The best approval ballot decides such a candidate against a lottery over the candidates above it, as in Proposition 5, not against the leader alone; exact best replies differ from the leader rule for a fifth to two fifths of random voters at every size tried (40 to 640 voters). A threshold against the lottery with the limiting weights agrees with the exact best reply more often (for 31 to 35 of 40 voters, against 23 to 32 for the leader rule) but not always: the exact weights also depend on which candidate is one vote ahead in a near tie.

**The Condorcet winner can lose.** Take candidates a₁, a₂ and c, and per 100 voters: 10 with c > a₁ > a₂; 42 with a₂ > c > a₁ and c rated at a quarter of the way from a₁ to a₂; 42 with a₁ > c > a₂ and c rated at two fifths of the way from a₂ to a₁; 4 with a₁ > a₂ > c; 2 with a₂ > a₁ > c. Then c beats a₁ and a₂ by 52 to 48, and a₁ beats a₂ by 56 to 44. Against the poll with a₁ leading, a₂ second and c at 10 per cent, the exact best replies reproduce the poll exactly for 100, 200, 400 and 800 voters. This is an equilibrium in Laslier's sense in which the Condorcet winner gets 10 per cent and a₁ is elected; under the leader rule c would get 52 per cent and overtake a₂. The camps that see c as a lukewarm compromise weigh it against both front-runners, and against both it loses. Laslier's Corollary 2 therefore does not hold in the model of the working paper as stated, where the order of ties is derived from uncounted marks. It is consistent with the published abstract if the order of ties there is assumed rather than derived: the example is an equilibrium of voters who compute the likelihood of ties from the model of mistakes, not of voters who hold (T).

**Sincerity.** With four or more candidates the exact best ballots are occasionally insincere: for random voters, 0 to 2 of 60 with four candidates and 2 of 60 with five, at 80 and 320 voters. One example, with five candidates ranked by score as listed and utilities (410, 601, 43, 404, 24), approves the runner-up and the fourth candidate and not the leader, whom the voter prefers to the fourth. The leader rule excludes exactly this. With three candidates the ballot is sincere, since the favourite is approved and the least preferred is not. Laslier's Corollary 1 (sincerity) therefore also rests on the half of his Lemma 2 that fails with uncounted marks.

**The walks with uncounted marks.** With the limiting weights for the candidates below the runner-up and voters responding to the previous tally, the walks of Sections 4a and 4b lose their guarantees (`scripts/information/lost_marks_walk.py`, 101 voters, starting from the plurality count). Under impartial culture, 10 to 21 per cent of the changes of leader go to a candidate that does not beat the old leader by majority, so Proposition 3, part 1, fails; the walk reaches the Condorcet winner in 88, 66 and 45 per cent of the profiles that have one, with three, four and five candidates, against all of them under the leader rule; and the walk with memory ends in the Smith set in 96, 89 and 85 per cent. In the spatial model the losses are small (Condorcet winner reached in 88 to 97 per cent, Smith set with memory in 98 to 99 per cent). Almost all failures are cycles, not polls that settle at another candidate: a self-confirming poll led by a candidate other than the Condorcet winner is rare from the plurality count (at most 3 in 400 profiles), though the example above shows it exists.

**Symmetric mistakes.** If a mark can also be added by mistake (with the same probability 1/20), a third candidate can rise as well as the others fall, and the ratios of its ties tend to zero, but slowly: the third candidate's tie with the runner-up weighs 0.047, 0.025 and 0.011 of its tie with the leader at 20, 80 and 320 voters. The example above is still an equilibrium at 100 and 200 voters and not at 400, where c's approvals jump to 208 per 400.

*Consequences.* The published framing separates two claims: an assumption about how voters weigh ties, and what follows from it. What follows is confirmed here (Propositions 1 and 2 under (T)). What is questioned is the assumption: independent recording errors that only lose marks do not support it for candidates ranked third or lower, and errors in both directions support it only in the limit, slowly. Whether a model of uncertainty that voters could plausibly hold yields (T) at realistic sizes is open (Section 9, question 9). What holds in both models of mistakes is everything that rests on the front-runners: parts 1 and 2 of Proposition 1 (equal tallies of the front-runners, the plurality ballot as a function of the approval ballot) and part 3 of Proposition 2 (every self-confirming approval poll confirms itself under plurality). The comparison of approval with plurality survives: approval's self-confirming polls are among plurality's. What depends on the kind of mistake, and on the size of the electorate, is that approval finds the Condorcet winner: with marks only uncounted it can fail in every electorate, with symmetric mistakes only in small ones. So the order of ties is not a technicality. It decides whether approval's extra information, the comparisons of the other candidates, is read against the leader or against a mixture of the front-runners, and only the first guarantees the Condorcet winner. *(Interpretation.)* The published version of Laslier's paper (2009) has not been compared with the working paper read here beyond its abstract.

## 5. Best and worst case

With Propositions 1 and 2 the two steps of Section 1 can be carried out, but the result depends on the target by which outcomes are judged.

**Majority as target.** If outcomes are judged by the majority relation (the Condorcet winner when there is one), approval's only self-confirming outcome is the best one, and it is also among plurality's. The best cases agree; in the worst case approval is strictly better whenever plurality has a self-confirming poll led by a candidate other than the Condorcet winner. In the computations that is the case in 71 percent of the profiles with a Condorcet winner for three candidates under impartial culture, and in nearly all of them otherwise. Plurality lies below approval. *(Proved, from Proposition 2.)*

**Total utility as target.** If outcomes are judged by the sum of utilities, the best case is no longer ordered. A plurality poll led by a candidate who is not the Condorcet winner but has a higher total utility can confirm itself, and approval cannot reach that candidate. In the computations this happens for about a quarter to a third of the profiles with a Condorcet winner under impartial culture, where utilities are unrelated across voters, and for under a tenth in the spatial model (*computed*). The worst case still favours approval as above.

*Reading.* Plurality's advantage in that case does not come from the electorate: the ballots carry the same single comparison whichever poll occurs, and the good outcome needs a fortunate poll. Under the common rationale neither method's ballots carry any information about intensities. Both reveal only who is preferred to whom, so a utilitarian target asks of them something neither delivers through the ballots. Two ways of stating the best case are possible, and the choice between them is open:

- the best over all self-confirming polls, which credits a method with information that arrives through the poll;
- the best use of the information carried by the ballots, which credits plurality with one comparison and approval with a row, and under which approval dominates for any target that depends only on the majority relation.

## 6. Score voting

Under the same rationale, score voting with scores 0 to k reduces to approval with every ballot multiplied by k (proved here: the tie between a₁ and a₂ forces the scores of the two to k and 0, and each tie with a₁ forces every other score to k or 0, according to the sign of u_x − u_{a₁}). Tallies are scaled by k, so outcomes and self-confirming polls coincide with those of approval. This is the case of (T) in the equivalence of Núñez and Laslier (2014): additive rules with the same extremal ballots are strategically equivalent in large elections, and the equivalence fails for small electorates (cited). Under this rationale score voting is exactly as good as approval and never worse, which is the first half of the claim that score voting dominates approval. The reduction does not need (T). For any weights of ties, as in Section 4c, the value of a score ballot is linear in the scores, Σ s(x) c(x), so a best ballot gives each candidate k or 0 according to the sign of its coefficient, and the best score ballot is k times the best approval ballot whenever no coefficient vanishes. *(Proved, from Proposition 5.)* An intermediate score can only be a best reply through what the linear valuation leaves out: near ties in which one candidate is a few points ahead, and the voter's own points as a share of a small electorate. In Laslier's binomial model with the others giving 0 or k points, exact best replies use an intermediate score as the unique best ballot for 5 or 6 of 50 random voters at 10 voters, for at most 1 of 50 from 20 voters on, and never from 40 voters on except once in 50 (with k = 2; three and four candidates; `scripts/information/score_exact.py`). With k = 3 no unique best ballot used an intermediate score: when every other voter gives 0 or k, the scores 1 to k − 1 all win a tie for their candidate without creating a new one, so they are interchangeable. *(Computed.)* So score voting carries no information beyond approval under the common rationale, except through the arithmetic of very small electorates.

## 7. What is assumed, and where it may break

- **The order of ties.** The whole comparison rests on (T), a large-electorate idealisation with distinct poll scores. In Laslier's model of mistakes (uncounted marks only) (T) holds for the front-runners and fails for the other candidates; with symmetric mistakes it holds in the limit, slowly (Section 4e). Without any order of ties, the best approval ballot is a threshold against a virtual opponent, a lottery over the other candidates weighted by the ties (Section 4c, Proposition 5); (T) is the corner in which all weight lies on one tie per candidate. Proposition 6 replaces the limit by a finite ratio, and Section 2a treats exact ties in the poll. The dependence of the weights on the tallies, which the walk needs, is not modelled away from the corner. In large Poisson games, a counterexample to the Condorcet consistency of approval voting is known (Núñez 2010†), so the conclusion of Section 4 depends on the strength of this assumption, not only on large numbers. Durand, Macé and Núñez (2019) study which equilibria of approval in Poisson games are stable and how often they elect the Condorcet winner.
- **A shared poll.** All voters respond to the same leader and runner-up. Polls that differ between groups of voters are not covered.
- **Ties.** Pairwise ties and ties between tallies are treated in Section 4d; only the strictness of defeats along the walk and, with random tie-breaking, the bound on rounds depend on them.
- **Strict preferences, a single winner, no abstention.** Indifferences change the leader rule at the margin; abstention is absent from both methods here.
- **Information in the poll.** Section 5 shows that the comparison depends on whether information arriving through the poll is counted. This is a question of definition, not of mathematics, and it should be settled before the framework is extended.
- **Other methods.** The rationale is stated for any method, but best replies under ranked methods (instant runoff, Condorcet methods) are not "one comparison per candidate", and Proposition 1 has no analogue yet. A ladder in which a well-played Condorcet method reveals the full matrix is plausible and unproved.

## 8. Connection with the Equality Criterion

Approval passes the Equality Criterion (an approval ballot and its complement add one approval to every candidate) and plurality fails it (*The Equality Criterion and Reversal*, Section 2). The criterion concerns sincere ballots. Proposition 5, part 3, carries it over to strategic ballots for approval: under the common rationale, with any weights of ties, a voter and the opposite voter cast complementary best ballots, so the pair changes no comparison between candidates. *(Proved.)* Under plurality the two best votes go to the candidates with the largest and the smallest coefficient. In the corner these are the two front-runners, and the pair cancels in their race. Away from it they need not belong to one race: with ties between a and b and between c and d both likely, a voter with a > b > c > d votes for a and the opposite voter for d, and the pair tilts both races (*computed example*).

So under the common rationale approval keeps equal and opposite votes for every poll, and plurality keeps them only when a single race dominates. In Proposition 1 the extra information of approval is the row of the leader, each entry a pairwise count on which a ballot and its opposite cancel. Whether failing the criterion always shows up as a choice of self-confirming poll made from outside the profile is open. *(Interpretation and question.)*

## 9. Open questions

1. Settle the definition of the best case (Section 5), and with it state the dominance order on methods in general.
2. Instant runoff and plurality under the same rationale; then a Condorcet method against approval.
3. Score against approval in small electorates, where intermediate scores can be best replies (Section 6): do they carry information that the tally uses, and does it improve the outcome?
4. Correlated uncertainty: Poisson populations, where Núñez (2010) finds a counterexample to the Condorcet consistency of approval; which condition of Proposition 6 fails there?
5. Measure the gap in the Ballot Front Finder Lab: the share of profiles in which the strategic outcome depends on the starting poll, per method.
6. The link with the Equality Criterion of Section 8, beyond Proposition 5, part 3.
7. A criterion in the spirit of the Smith set: a method reaches the Smith set under the common rationale within N rounds from every poll. Approval with the leader rule fails it (Section 4a), rarely; with memory of who has led it holds with N = 2(m − 1) (Section 4b). Which other methods satisfy it, and is 2(m − 1) the right bound for a rule that does not stop?
8. The walk away from the corner: a model of how the weights of ties follow from the tallies, and with it the self-confirming polls and the walk for arbitrary weights. With a continuum of voters and weights that depend continuously on the tallies, Brouwer's theorem would give a self-confirming poll for every profile; where it sits when there is no Condorcet winner is the question.
9. The kind of ballot mistake (Section 4e): which models of mistakes or of uncertainty about the tallies give (T), and how fast? With marks only uncounted, the walks lose their guarantees (computed); what are all the equilibria of approval there, and is there a rule for publishing polls that restores the Smith set?
