abstract: |
  We present Consensus Or Random Exclusion (CORE), a procedure for groups that have to agree on a single course of action. The participants deliberate toward unanimous agreement, and at any time any of them may call for one participant, drawn uniformly at random and possibly the caller, to be removed. If removals continue, the procedure ends in random dictatorship, so that each participant's fallback is a fair lottery over the participants' ideals, which we call their endowment. A call never changes anyone's expected endowment, and it has the same effect whoever makes it. The lottery gives weight only to first choices, so a compromise that everyone values and nobody ranks first can be reached only by agreement. CORE is therefore not an algorithm that computes a result from ballots but a frame for bargaining: deliberation, which we leave without formal rules on purpose, is the search for such outcomes and the bargaining over how to share what they gain. No participant and no group can force an outcome. All that anyone can force is the step from deliberation to the draw. When smaller groups can agree after a removal, an agreement survives the refusal of a few, but value can shift toward or away from those removed, and we state the guarantees that hold regardless: every participant keeps a $1/n$ chance of their ideal, and any $m$ participants keep a chance of $m/n$ for an option they share. A decision covers only what its participants brought into it. We discuss who should take part, interpret the mechanism as a veil of uncertainty over who will be decisive, relate it to the consensus methods of Heitzig and Simmons, and sketch extensions to sortition and delegation.

## 1. Introduction

Suppose a group has to agree on a single course of action, and its members want different things. The three familiar ways of settling the matter each fall short. Majority rule lets the larger side impose its will on the smaller. Strict unanimity looks like the fair alternative, but it lets any single member block a decision indefinitely, and blocking keeps things as they are. Whoever benefits from the way things are can therefore block everyone else. Unanimity does not avoid imposition. It hands the power to impose to the status quo. A random lot treats everyone alike, but it cannot find an agreement even when one exists.

In this paper we present Consensus Or Random Exclusion (CORE), a fourth way of reaching a decision, which combines the search for unanimous agreement with the fairness of a lottery. It consists of two moves:

1. The group deliberates, looking for a proposal that everyone accepts.
2. At any point, any participant may call for one participant to be removed from the deliberation. The person removed is drawn uniformly at random from everyone present, including the one who called. The remaining participants then continue with step 1.

This is the whole mechanism. The rest of the paper explains why it works, what exactly it guarantees, and where its guarantees end.

The first move has no further rules. It is not a vote, and nothing fixes how proposals are made or in what order they are taken up. CORE is therefore not a voting procedure that computes a result from ballots. It is a frame for bargaining. The frame fixes two things: when a decision has been reached, and what happens if none is. Within it the participants search for an agreement and bargain over its terms, and all of them have the same fallback and the same moves. The absence of further rules is not an omission. Section 9.5 argues that any formal rule for deliberation would build an exploitable advantage into the procedure and would confine the search to options someone had thought of before it began. Deliberation has to be left open.

### Building intuition

It helps to start from the case in which agreement never comes. If participants keep calling for removals, the group shrinks one member at a time until a single participant is left, and that participant's preferred outcome is enacted. Because every removal is a uniform draw, each participant is equally likely to be the one left at the end (equivalent to random ballot).

As long as no agreement is reached, then, each of the $k$ participants present has a $1/k$ chance of being the one who decides. What a participant holds in this situation is a fair lottery over everyone's preferred outcomes, and we call its value to them their **endowment**. It rests on a probability of being decisive, not on a share of a vote, and it is the fair benchmark against which every agreement is measured.

Two properties of the endowment make the mechanism work. The first is that calling for a removal does not change its value. A participant who calls may survive into a smaller group, where their chance rises from $1/k$ to $1/(k-1)$, or may be the one removed. In expectation these two possibilities cancel exactly, and they cancel for every other participant as well (Theorem 1). This is also the reason the caller has to stand in their own draw. If callers were exempt, calling would become profitable, and the participants furthest from the rest of the group would call constantly (Corollary 1.3).

The second property is that the endowment is usually a poor deal. A lottery over everyone's preferred outcomes is a gamble between extremes. Most of its weight falls on outcomes that someone else wants most, and those are often much worse for you than a reasonable compromise would be. There is therefore usually room for an outcome that every participant prefers to their own endowment at the same time.

This gap is what drives the mechanism. Deliberation searches for an outcome that beats the lottery for everyone at once, and unanimous agreement is the exchange in which each participant gives up their chance of getting everything in return for something they prefer to that chance. The lottery cannot find such outcomes, because it counts only first choices. An outcome receives exactly the share of participants who rank it first, however much everyone else may value it, so a compromise that nobody ranks first has no chance at all (Theorem 3). Whatever a group gains by agreeing, it gains by giving some outcome more weight than its share of first choices, and only deliberation can do that. This is why deliberation is the substance of the procedure. It is also why the mechanism has an honest limit: where the disagreement is purely about dividing something fixed and no compromise exists, there is nothing to find, and CORE returns the fair lottery (Proposition 1).

This gap is also the reason anyone should take part. The fairness results that follow describe how the fallback is shared, and it is easy to read them as if the point of the mechanism were only to stop anyone from gaining an unfair advantage. But nobody joins a decision merely to avoid being cheated. People join because coming together can leave everyone better off than they would be apart. CORE is designed so that every participant can look for this gain from the same position, with the same fallback and the same moves. The guarantees are the floor. The reason to take part is what can be gained above it (Section 8.1).

Usually more than one outcome beats the lottery for everyone, and the participants then also have to settle which one they adopt. That part is a bargaining problem. Everyone shares an interest in reaching some agreement, and they differ over which, so the gain from agreement is collective while its division is contested. Section 9.4 discusses how this bargaining can converge, and why no rule can settle it in advance.

One complication should be met early. The picture so far assumes that once removals begin, they continue to the end. But a smaller group is still a group, and after a removal it may find an agreement of its own. A removed participant then no longer receives the lottery over the remaining preferred outcomes. They receive whatever the remaining participants agree on. That may be worse for them than the lottery, if the others share a compromise they dislike, or better, if the compromise suits them. Section 7 works this out and distinguishes two variants of the mechanism. In the sequential variant, which is the one described above, smaller groups can find agreements the lottery would miss, and those agreements can shift value toward or away from the participants who were removed. In the one-shot variant, a stalled deliberation goes directly to the lottery, so that the endowment is exactly what everyone holds. The variants also differ in what a single refusal can do. In the one-shot variant, one participant who refuses sends everyone to the lottery, and in a large group it is almost certain that someone will. In the sequential variant, a participant who refuses is likely to be removed, and the others can still agree. For this reason we recommend the sequential variant for all but small groups. Both variants keep a core of guarantees.

With these ideas in place, three consequences can be stated before the formal development.

The first is that the removals are not meant to happen. The uniform lottery over the participants' preferred outcomes is itself a proposal the group can adopt, and it leaves everyone exactly at their endowment. The possibility of removal does its work by being available, since it fixes what any agreement has to beat (Theorem 2).

The second is that no participant holds a special instrument. The only move anyone can make alone is the call, and a call triggers the same random event no matter who makes it. A majority has no lever that a minority lacks. Every participant can secure a $1/n$ chance that their preferred outcome is enacted, and any $m$ participants acting together can secure a chance of $m/n$ for an option they share (Section 7).

The third concerns the reach of a decision. A decision reached under CORE is valid only among those who took part in it, and only over what they brought into it. It cannot authorize anything beyond that. People outside the group who are affected by what the group does keep their claims, and the way to settle those claims is a wider agreement that includes them (Section 8). We deliberately do not protect everyone's current position as such, because doing so would give the status quo exactly the veto we objected to at the start.

### Where it could be used

CORE is a natural fit for situations that already call for unanimity, or for something close to it, and in which unanimity regularly ends in deadlock. NATO takes its decisions by consensus among its members, and the Council of the European Union still requires unanimity in areas such as foreign policy, defence and taxation. In both, a single member can hold up a decision that all the others support, and the fallback is that nothing happens. Citizens' assemblies usually deliberate at length and then close with a vote, which hands the last word back to a majority; CORE could take the place of that vote. Siblings who inherit a house together and can sell it only if all of them agree can be stuck for years, and the legal fallback is often a court-ordered sale that serves none of them well. In each case CORE keeps unanimity as the goal and replaces the deadlock with a fallback that treats everyone alike.

Two cautions apply. The first is that the uniform lottery is the right fallback only where the default is meant to be neutral among the options. A criminal jury, which in several legal systems also has to be unanimous, shows where it is not. The presumption of innocence deliberately favors the defendant, and a fallback that convicted with a probability equal to the share of jurors favoring conviction would abandon it. A jury settling the amount of damages, by contrast, is a natural case.

The second caution is that the members of such a body have to accept the commitment CORE requires, which includes being bound by a decision reached after they were removed. They also have to accept it in advance, for a whole class of decisions. A member who is free to decide case by case whether a question goes to CORE keeps the veto, since they will decline whenever the present arrangement suits them better than the lottery (Section 8.1). For the siblings, the commitment would have to come from the will or from the law of inheritance. For sovereign states it is a large step, and Section 13.3 discusses why such commitments are hard to obtain.

Section 8 discusses why people take part and what a decision can cover. Section 10 asks who is entitled to take part in a decision. Section 11 offers an interpretation of the mechanism as a veil of uncertainty. Section 12 relates CORE to existing work, in particular to the non-deterministic consensus methods of Heitzig and Simmons, which use the same benchmark. Section 13 sketches how the mechanism extends to sortition and delegation, and Section 14 collects the problems that remain open.

*A note for readers of the earlier presentation.* An earlier version of this work (Kōmyō and Claude 2025) defined each participant's acceptance threshold by a recursion over subsets of participants. The recursion assumed that each subset reaches an agreement whenever one is available, but it did not say which agreement. Since that choice changes every value computed from it, the threshold was not well defined. Here we use the endowment as the benchmark, which requires no such choice. This does not make the underlying question disappear. In the sequential variant, what participants actually hold still depends on which agreements smaller groups would reach, and we leave that as an open equilibrium question (Section 14). What we offer instead are guarantees that hold whatever those agreements are.

Three claims of the earlier work are withdrawn. Whether to accept a proposal is not a decision that can be made without regard to what others will do. The endowment tells a participant what they need not accept, and beyond that the decision belongs to bargaining (Section 9.4). An addendum on strategic manipulation (Kōmyō and Claude 2026) claimed that every participant can guarantee their endowment whatever the others do, which fails in the sequential variant (Section 7.5). And the earlier work called the mechanism Rawlsian, a label we no longer use (Section 11.1). Readers who are new to CORE can pass over this note, since the paper does not otherwise refer to the earlier versions.

## 2. The mechanism

Let $V$ be a finite set of $n$ participants. Each participant brings something into the decision, such as money into a fund, their own usage into a technical standard, or their labor into a project. We call everything brought in the **pool**, and we write $\mathcal{A}$ for the set of outcomes that can be realized by acting on the pool. Section 8 discusses what this means. For now it is enough that $\mathcal{A}$ contains only what the participants have at their disposal.

Each participant $i$ evaluates outcomes by a von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function $u_i : \mathcal{A} \to \mathbb{R}$, which extends to lotteries over $\mathcal{A}$ by taking expectations. We write $a^*_i$ for an outcome that maximizes $u_i$ over $\mathcal{A}$, call it $i$'s **ideal**, and write $t_i = u_i(a^*_i)$ for its value to $i$. We assume that such an outcome exists, as it does when $\mathcal{A}$ is finite, or compact with continuous utilities. If several outcomes are equally good for $i$, we take $a^*_i$ to be the one $i$ would enact if left alone. Because ideals are taken within $\mathcal{A}$, they are always feasible: a participant's ideal is the best that can be done with the pool, not the best they can imagine.

Proposals may be lotteries. This matters for the substance of the mechanism, because the one proposal a group can always agree on is a lottery, as Section 5 shows.

On a current set $S$ of participants, the procedure runs as follows.

1. Participants deliberate and may propose any outcome in $\mathcal{A}$ or any lottery over $\mathcal{A}$.
2. If every member of $S$ accepts a proposal, it is enacted and the process ends.
3. Any member of $S$ may call for a removal. One member of $S$ is then drawn uniformly at random, possibly the caller, and removed. The process continues on the reduced set.
4. If only one participant remains, their ideal is enacted.

Two features of this description are easily misread. The first is that step 3 is not triggered by a failure of step 2. Deliberation and the call are independent of each other, and both are available at any time: a participant may call for a removal while a promising proposal is under discussion, and a group may go on deliberating long after its first proposals have failed. The second is that the steps do not describe a vote. Nothing in CORE puts proposals to the group one at a time, to be accepted or rejected in turn. As soon as proposals are taken up in a fixed order, the order itself favors some of them over others. Deliberation is instead an open search that may arrive at an outcome everyone accepts, and it ends when the participants recognize that they have found one. It has no formal rules, because any such rule would set a constraint that could be exploited (Section 9.5). The language of proposals and acceptance serves only to model the result of deliberation. It does not prescribe how deliberation proceeds.

We call the procedure above the **sequential** mechanism. It has a **one-shot** variant, in which a call removes participants until only one remains, all in a single step, so that a stalled deliberation goes directly to the uniform lottery over the participants' ideals. The one-shot variant is essentially the method that Heitzig and Simmons (2012) call "Full consensus or Random Ballot fall-back". The two variants differ only in whether smaller groups get a chance to agree along the way. Section 7 examines what that difference costs and what it buys.

Three further remarks clarify what kind of mechanism this is.

First, invoking CORE is itself a commitment to reach a single outcome. One might ask why a group that disagrees does not simply split, with each faction carrying out its own plan. The answer is that splitting is not an alternative within CORE but the ungoverned situation CORE starts from. People can always come together or not, agree or not, and regroup, and doing so costs nothing. Splitting reveals which groups already exist, but it produces nothing beyond that. The difference shows up in what each participant falls back on. When splitting is the fallback, each participant measures a compromise against what their own faction can do alone, and whoever does well alone has no reason to move. Under CORE the fallback is the lottery, which is worse for each participant than a good agreement, and it is this ordering that makes agreement worthwhile. Everything CORE adds to ungoverned interaction lies in the commitment to produce one outcome and adopt it.

Second, a removal takes away a participant's voice but not their stake. A removed participant takes no further part in deliberating or in accepting proposals, but what they brought into the pool stays there, and they receive whatever the remaining group enacts. This follows from the commitment just described, since a participant who could escape the outcome by being removed would never have been committed to it. It also has a cost, which Section 7 makes precise: what a removed participant brought can be disposed of by the others.

Third, every agreement is an interim agreement. Reaching one settles the decision for now, not forever. Circumstances change, and an agreement may later need to be adjusted, which is done by a new process of the same kind, starting from the situation the earlier agreement created. In that new process the earlier agreement is not protected as such, for the same reason that nobody's current position is (Section 8.3). Within a single deliberation, too, the participants may agree on an interim result without ending the search: they may adopt a lottery that everyone prefers to the current fallback, and continue looking for something better. Such a step needs no rule of its own, since it is one more thing a group can agree on unanimously. From then on the adopted lottery is what the group has decided, and the continued search is a search for a unanimous amendment to it. Unless the agreement says otherwise, a call at this stage no longer removes anyone. It ends the search, and the adopted lottery is carried out. In the continued search every participant is therefore pivotal again, as in the one-shot variant (Section 7.3), which is the price of having locked in the gain. Section 9.4 shows why such steps matter for how bargaining converges.

## 3. The endowment

**Definition.** For a participant $i$ in $V$ and a set $S$ of participants, the endowment of $i$ at $S$ is

$$e_i(S) = \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{j \in S} u_i(a^*_j)$$

It is the value to $i$ of the uniform lottery over the ideals of the members of $S$. Equivalently, it is what $i$ receives under random dictatorship on $S$, and what $i$ receives if removals continue from $S$ until one participant is left without any agreement being reached. The definition applies whether or not $i$ belongs to $S$. This matters, because a removed participant still receives the outcome.

Heitzig and Simmons (2012) use the same quantity as their benchmark. They call the uniform lottery over the voters' favorites the benchmark lottery and define potential consensus options as the options everyone prefers to it.

Three properties of the endowment are used throughout. It is selection-free: computing $e_i(S)$ requires only the participants' ideals, with no assumption about which agreement any group would reach or about anyone's strategy. It is the value of something the procedure actually does: if removals run to the end without agreement, a single participant remains and their ideal is enacted, and since a sequence of uniform removals leaves each member of $S$ equally likely to be the survivor, $e_i(S)$ is exactly the value of this outcome to $i$. And it is a benchmark rather than a guarantee. In the one-shot variant the endowment is exactly what each participant holds before an agreement is reached. In the sequential variant it is not, because smaller groups may reach agreements that change what everyone receives, whether present or removed. Section 7 shows that this difference can go in either direction and can be large, and it states what each participant is guaranteed in every case.

## 4. The call for random exclusion

The call for a removal is the only move a participant can make alone. We first record a fact that holds exactly in both variants, and then a fact about the call measured against the benchmark.

**Observation (Caller independence).** The effect of a call does not depend on who makes it. Every call removes a uniformly random member of $S$, and the process continues from the reduced set without any record of who called.

Simple as it is, this observation carries a lot of weight. No participant or group has an instrument that others lack. Whatever advantage a call gives anyone, it gives them equally whether they made the call themselves or their opponent did.

**Theorem 1 (Fair call).** Let $|S| = k \geq 2$ and consider a single call at $S$. For every $i$ in $V$, the expected endowment after the call equals $e_i(S)$.

*Proof.* Write $T = \sum_{j \in S} u_i(a^*_j)$, so that $e_i(S) = T/k$. With probability $1/k$ the removed participant is $j$, which leaves $S \setminus \{j\}$ with $e_i(S \setminus \{j\}) = (T - u_i(a^*_j))/(k-1)$. This holds whether or not $j = i$, since a removed participant still receives the outcome. The expectation is

$$\frac{1}{k} \sum_{j \in S} \frac{T - u_i(a^*_j)}{k-1} = \frac{kT - T}{k(k-1)} = \frac{T}{k} = e_i(S).$$

The argument uses no property of the utilities. $\square$

A call is therefore a fair gamble in endowments. It spreads them out without moving their average, and it transfers nothing, to the caller or to anyone else. Since the utilities already contain each participant's attitude to risk, a gamble that is fair in utility is neither good nor bad for them: measured against the benchmark, a call leaves everyone exactly where they were in expectation. Where risk does make a difference is in the comparison between the lottery and a compromise, which Section 6 takes up. In the one-shot variant the theorem is a statement about what participants actually hold. In the sequential variant it says only that calling does not move the benchmark, and Section 7 shows that a call can move what participants actually hold.

**Corollary 1.1 (Any number of calls).** The expected endowment after any sequence of calls equals $e_i(S)$, whoever makes the calls and however the decision to call again depends on who has been removed so far. In particular, removals that run to the end are worth $e_i(S)$ to every participant.

*Proof.* By Theorem 1, the expected endowment after each further call equals the endowment before it, whatever has happened up to that point. Since at most $k - 1$ calls can be made, the claim follows by induction on the number of calls. $\square$

**Corollary 1.2 (No gain against the benchmark).** No participant can raise their endowment by calling. In the one-shot variant, a participant can therefore always refuse an offer worth less than their endowment. Calling instead costs them nothing in expectation and secures exactly their endowment, so that nobody has reason to accept less. It does not secure more. What a participant obtains above their endowment depends on what the others are willing to concede, and each of them holds the same threat (Section 9.4).

**Corollary 1.3 (The caller must stand in the draw).** Suppose instead that callers were exempt, so that one of the other $k-1$ participants is removed. Write $C_i(S')$ for the value to $i$ of continuing from a set $S'$, whatever the participants actually do there. Exemption then changes the caller's expected value by

$$\frac{1}{k} \Big[ \frac{1}{k-1} \sum_{j \neq i} C_i(S \setminus \{j\}) - C_i(S \setminus \{i\}) \Big]$$

that is, by the difference between the caller's average value when surviving and their value when removed, divided by $k$. Measured in endowments, where $C_i = e_i$, this equals

$$\frac{t_i - e_i(S)}{(k-1)^2}$$

which is never negative, and positive for anyone who is not indifferent among the ideals present.

*Proof.* With the caller standing in the draw, the caller's expected value is $(1/k) \sum_{j \in S} C_i(S \setminus \{j\})$. With the caller exempt, it is $(1/(k-1)) \sum_{j \neq i} C_i(S \setminus \{j\})$. Subtracting the first from the second gives the stated expression. For endowments, $(1/(k-1)) \sum_{j \neq i} e_i(S \setminus \{j\}) = ((k-2)T + t_i)/(k-1)^2$ and $e_i(S \setminus \{i\}) = (T - t_i)/(k-1)$, with $T$ as in Theorem 1. Substituting gives $(k t_i - T)/(k(k-1)^2) = (t_i - e_i(S))/(k-1)^2$, which is nonnegative because $t_i$ is at least every $u_i(a^*_j)$. $\square$

Measured against the benchmark, then, exempting callers would make calling profitable for almost everyone, and most of all for those whose ideal lies furthest from the rest of the group, who would call constantly. Exemption would also turn the call into a race. A participant who cannot be removed by their own calls is the more likely to be left at the end the more often they call, and one who called before anyone else each time would be certain of it. Requiring callers to stand in their own draw is what makes the only individual move neutral with respect to who makes it. In the sequential variant the general expression is typically positive as well, but this is not guaranteed. It can be negative when the others would agree on something the participant likes once the participant is gone, but would not accept it while the participant is present, because the participant's ideal gives them a gamble that they prefer and the participant fears. Such cases are special. In the ordinary case, the compromise the others would reach without a participant is also on offer while that participant is present, and the participant can accept it or ask for more.

## 5. Consensus and the fallback

**Theorem 2 (The lottery as a proposal).** Let $L(S)$ be the uniform lottery over $\{a^*_j : j \in S\}$. Then $u_i(L(S)) = e_i(S)$ for every $i$.

*Proof.* This follows directly from the definition of the endowment and the expected-utility extension. $\square$

In the one-shot variant, $L(S)$ is exactly what a stalled deliberation produces. There is therefore always a proposal that everyone weakly accepts, and adopting $L(S)$ by agreement is at least as good for everyone as letting the deliberation stall, since both lead to the same distribution over outcomes. The fallback does its work counterfactually: it fixes what any agreement has to beat, without needing to occur.

Heitzig and Simmons (2012) prove the matching equilibrium result for their method. Using a strong equilibrium concept in which coalitions can coordinate their deviations, they show that whenever some option is preferred by everyone to the benchmark lottery, the equilibria are exactly the efficient ways of electing such options by full consensus, so that no equilibrium outcome is worse for anyone than the benchmark. Their result concerns a fixed set of options and one-shot ballots rather than open deliberation, but its structure is that of our one-shot variant. It supplies something we do not prove independently here: that agreement is not merely available but an equilibrium, and that there is no equilibrium of any other kind. Whether participants arrive at it is a further question, since with several such options they still have to settle on one (Section 9.4).

In the sequential variant, an analogous proposal exists but is no longer selection-free. Consider the lottery over outcomes that a call would actually lead to, given whatever agreements smaller groups would reach. It gives each participant exactly what they would hold after a call, so everyone weakly accepts it too. But what that lottery is depends on which agreements smaller groups would reach, which is precisely the question the endowment was introduced to avoid. And $L(S)$ itself is no longer guaranteed to be acceptable, since participants who expect to do well in smaller groups will reject it (Section 7).

## 6. Where the gains from agreement lie

**Theorem 3 (Support).** The fallback lottery gives each outcome a probability equal to the share of participants whose ideal it is. In particular, an outcome that is nobody's ideal has no chance under the fallback and can be reached only by agreement.

*Proof.* The fallback enacts the ideal of the last remaining participant, and each participant is equally likely to be the last. $\square$

This tells us where the value of deliberation lies. The lottery counts first choices and nothing else. How much the other participants value an outcome plays no role in the weight it receives, so whatever a group gains by agreeing rather than gambling, it gains by giving some outcome more weight than its share of first choices. This may be a compromise that nobody ranks first and that the lottery never selects, or one participant's ideal that the others also value highly. In both cases the gain is invisible to the lottery and can be found only through the search that deliberation performs.

An example of Dutta, Peters and Sen (2007) shows how much is at stake. A thousand participants each have a different favorite among a thousand and one options, and every one of them ranks the remaining option second. Suppose each values their own favorite at 1, the common second choice at 0.9, and everyone else's favorite at 0. The lottery over ideals is worth 0.001 to each participant, and it never selects the option that everyone ranks second, while agreement on that option is worth 0.9 to each.

The example also shows why unanimity matters for reaching such an option. Under the COWPEA Lottery of Pereira (2023), a random ballot is drawn and only the candidates it approves are kept, further random ballots remove the candidates they do not approve, and a ballot that would remove all remaining candidates is ignored. If every participant approves their favorite and the common second choice, the second choice is elected for certain. But a participant who approves only their favorite still gets the second choice whenever another ballot is drawn first, and gets their favorite in the one case in a thousand in which their own ballot comes first. That is worth 0.9001 rather than 0.9, so every participant gains by withholding approval of the compromise, and if all of them do, the result is the lottery again. The compromise is a public good on which each participant can free-ride.

In the one-shot variant of CORE this cannot happen. The compromise is enacted only if every participant accepts it, so refusing it does not let anyone keep the compromise and add a chance of their favorite. Without their acceptance there is no compromise, and what remains is the lottery, worth 0.001. Unanimity makes every participant pivotal, and a pivotal participant has nothing to free-ride on. A participant who wants more has to ask the others for it, which turns free-riding into open bargaining (Section 9.4). Heitzig, Simmons and Constantino (2025) identify the same free-rider problem and address it in MaxParC with conditional commitments to approve compromise options.

Being pivotal has a price, however, since a participant without whom there is no compromise can also hold it up. In the sequential variant both effects are weaker. Section 7.3 compares the variants in this respect.

Theorem 3 also explains why CORE is not an aggregation rule in the classical sense. An aggregation rule maps a profile of preferences over a fixed set of alternatives to an element of that set. In CORE the gains may lie in alternatives that were on no list at the outset, and generating them is part of what the procedure does. Impossibility theorems about aggregation over a fixed set of alternatives therefore do not describe CORE. This is not a way of evading those theorems but a description of what kind of object CORE is, and Section 9 discusses the results that do apply.

The advantage of agreement over the lottery is conditional, and the condition is worth stating plainly.

**Proposition 1 (Zero-sum reduction).** If no lottery over $\mathcal{A}$ is weakly preferred to $L(S)$ by every member of $S$ and strictly preferred by at least one, then every lottery that all members of $S$ weakly prefer to $L(S)$ gives each of them exactly their endowment, and agreement can do no better than the fallback. This is the case, for instance, when two participants face two outcomes on which their preferences are exactly opposed, with utilities normalized to 0 and 1. Every lottery then gives the two participants utilities that sum to 1, and CORE reduces to random dictatorship.

*Proof.* The first claim restates the assumption. In the example, a lottery that places probability $p$ on the first participant's preferred outcome gives the two participants utilities $p$ and $1 - p$, so no lottery makes both better off than $(1/2, 1/2)$. $\square$

The proposition marks the scope of what CORE can do. The advantage of consensus over randomness extends exactly as far as compromise is possible. Where the conflict is purely about how to divide something fixed, deliberation has nothing to find and the mechanism returns the fair lottery. Where compromise exists, deliberation is the only way to reach it.

At the other end stands a large class of decisions in which a gain is certain to exist.

**Observation (Averaging beats the lottery).** Suppose $\mathcal{A}$ is a convex set, so that outcomes can be averaged, as with the divisions of a budget or the positions on a scale, and suppose every $u_i$ is concave on it. Let

$$\bar{a}(S) = \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{j \in S} a^*_j$$

be the average of the ideals. Then $u_i(\bar{a}(S)) \geq e_i(S)$ for every $i$. The inequality is strict for every participant whose utility is strictly concave, unless all the ideals coincide.

*Proof.* By Jensen's inequality, the value of an average is at least the average of the values:

$$u_i\Big(\frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{j \in S} a^*_j\Big) \geq \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{j \in S} u_i(a^*_j) = e_i(S).$$

$\square$

For a participant who maximizes expected utility, concavity of $u_i$ is what aversion to risk over outcomes means. The observation therefore states the precise sense in which risk aversion favors agreement. Participants who dislike gambling over outcomes all prefer the average of the ideals for certain to the lottery over them. For a budget, the average of the ideals is the outcome in which each participant directs an equal share of the pool. It is a compromise that needs no negotiation and already beats the lottery for everyone, though a group can usually do better still. When utilities are linear, as they are over the lotteries in the example of Proposition 1, the inequality holds with equality and averaging gains nothing.

## 7. When smaller groups agree

### 7.1 Benchmark and holding can come apart

After each removal, the sequential mechanism gives the smaller group another chance to agree. That is its purpose: an agreement that was impossible with a certain participant present may become possible without them, and the one-shot variant would forgo it. But the same feature changes what every participant actually holds.

We illustrate this with three participants and four outcomes. $A$, $B$ and $C$ are the ideals of participants 1, 2 and 3. $D$ is a compromise that participants 2 and 3 both value highly and that is ruinous for participant 1.

\newpage

| | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $u_1$ | 10 | 5 | 5 | -100 |
| $u_2$ | 0 | 10 | 0 | 9 |
| $u_3$ | 0 | 0 | 10 | 9 |

We ask what each participant can expect if someone calls for a removal at the full group. Each of the three is removed with probability $1/3$, and we work through the three cases in turn.

If participant 1 is removed, participants 2 and 3 are left as a pair. For each of them, the pair's lottery gives a half chance of their own ideal, worth 10, and a half chance of the other's ideal, worth 0, so each holds an endowment of 5. Outcome $D$ gives each of them 9, and they agree on it. In fact any lottery that both of them strictly prefer to their endowment has to put some weight on $D$, so participant 1 loses from any agreement the pair reaches; we take $D$ itself for concreteness. Participant 1 receives -100, and participants 2 and 3 receive 9 each.

If participant 2 is removed, participants 1 and 3 are left. No lottery is better for both of them than a half chance of $A$ and a half chance of $C$, so they cannot agree, removals continue, and one of them is left at the end with equal probability. The outcome is $A$ or $C$, each with probability $1/2$, which is worth 7.5 to participant 1, 0 to participant 2 and 5 to participant 3. The case in which participant 3 is removed is symmetric, with $A$ or $B$ as the outcome.

Averaging over the three cases gives each participant's expected value after a call. For participant 1 it is $(-100 + 7.5 + 7.5)/3$, for participant 2 it is $(9 + 0 + 5)/3$, and participant 3 is symmetric to participant 2:

| | endowment $e_i(V)$ | expected value after a call |
|---|---|---|
| participant 1 | 6.67 | -28.33 |
| participant 2 | 3.33 | 4.67 |
| participant 3 | 3.33 | 4.67 |

The comparison shows three things. Participant 1 holds far less than their endowment. Participants 2 and 3 hold more than theirs, and so they will reject $L(V)$, which offers them only their endowment. And a call, although it leaves every endowment unchanged (Theorem 1), changes what the participants actually hold. It takes 35 from participant 1 and gives 1.33 to each of the others, so most of what participant 1 loses is destroyed rather than transferred.

These values are what a call is worth, not what the participants end up with. Because a call is so costly for participant 1, the full group has agreements that all three prefer to it. An even chance of $B$ and $C$, for instance, gives each of them 5. The harm to participant 1 therefore shows up as a weak position in the bargaining of the full group, which starts from $-28.33$ for participant 1 and from 4.67 for the others, and not from the endowments.

The effect is not specific to this example. Whenever the groups that can form after a removal share compromises that the removed participant dislikes, the sequential mechanism shifts value toward those who can agree with the others and away from those who cannot. The advantage does not belong to a majority as such, and it cannot be seized by whoever calls, since calls are caller-independent. It belongs to whoever is compatible with whoever remains. In a group with a like-minded bloc, however, that will usually be the bloc.

### 7.2 The effect can also run the other way

The same mechanism can work in the opposite direction. Change the example so that participant 1 values $D$ at 6 and the other two ideals $B$ and $C$ at 0, leaving everything else as before. When participant 1 is removed, participants 2 and 3 again agree on $D$, and participant 1 now receives 6, where the lottery over the remaining ideals would have given them nothing. This does not depend on which agreement the pair chooses: as before, any agreement both of them strictly prefer to their endowment puts weight on $D$, and every such agreement is better for participant 1 than the lottery over $B$ and $C$.

Being removed is not an advantage here. $D$ is worth more than the endowment to all three participants, 6 against 3.33 for participant 1 and 9 against 3.33 for the others, so the full group can agree on it without any removal. And if another participant is removed, participant 1 is left in a pair in which $D$ again beats the lottery for both, and can accept it or ask for more. What the example shows is that participant 1 is well placed either way. Removed, they fall back on an agreement that suits them. Present, they bargain from a fallback that is better than their endowment: with $D$ as the pair's agreement, a call is worth at least $(6 + 5 + 5)/3 = 5.33$ to participant 1, against an endowment of 3.33.

Which direction prevails depends on the preferences involved. A compromise among the remaining participants can be worse for someone outside it than the lottery over their ideals, as in the first example, or better, as here. The mechanism itself favors neither direction. What it changes is that the removed participant no longer receives the lottery, but whatever those who remain can agree on.

### 7.3 Free-riding and hold-up

The two variants differ in a second respect, namely in what a single participant can do by refusing. Take again the example of Section 6, in which each of a thousand participants values their own favorite at 1, a common second choice at 0.9, and everyone else's favorite at 0. We write $Q$ for the common second choice.

In the one-shot variant every participant is pivotal. Section 6 showed the benefit, which is that nobody can free-ride on a compromise that does not exist without them. The price is that every participant holds a veto over the whole gain from agreement. If a single participant refuses $Q$ and the deliberation stalls, everyone receives the lottery, worth 0.001 instead of 0.9. Carrying out such a refusal costs the refuser as much as it costs everyone else, so no participant has reason to do it lightly, and by the equilibrium result of Heitzig and Simmons (2012) no participant or coalition gains by departing from the consensus. The exposure is to participants who refuse anyway, out of conviction, by mistake, or because they have staked their credibility on a demand. Such a demand can be large, since the others would still prefer to the fallback a lottery that gives the refuser's favorite a weight of up to about 0.999 and $Q$ the rest. The exposure also grows with the size of the group. If each of the thousand participants independently refuses with a probability of one in a thousand, the one-shot variant reaches the compromise only about 37 percent of the time. This is the veto of strict unanimity in a milder form. Blocking no longer keeps things as they are, but it still destroys what agreement would have gained.

The sequential variant dilutes this power. Consider a participant $h$ who refuses every proposal and keeps calling, while the others are willing to adopt $Q$ among themselves whenever at least two of them remain. If $h$ is removed while two or more others are left, they adopt $Q$, and $h$ receives it anyway. If $h$ is the last one left, which happens with probability $1/n$, $h$ enacts their own ideal. Only if $h$ is removed when a single other participant is left, which also happens with probability $1/n$, does $h$ lose the compromise, since that participant then enacts their own ideal.

The others lose little by this. $Q$ is enacted with probability $(n-2)/n$, here 0.998, and each of them receives 0.8982 instead of 0.9. The most $h$ can extract by threatening to hold out is correspondingly small. The others have no reason to concede more than the holding out would cost them, which limits the weight they would give to $h$'s favorite to about $2/n$, here 0.002. The same reasoning covers several refusers at once.

**Observation (Robustness to refusal).** Suppose $d$ of the $n$ participants refuse every proposal and keep calling, while the others adopt $Q$ as soon as no refuser is present and at least two of them remain. Then $Q$ is enacted with probability

$$\frac{(n-d)(n-d-1)}{n(n-1)}.$$

*Proof.* Removals continue for as long as a refuser is present. $Q$ is therefore enacted exactly when the two participants who would be the last to remain, if removals ran to the end, are both willing to adopt it. Since every removal is a uniform draw, these two are a uniformly random pair. $\square$

With ten refusers among the thousand participants, the probability is still 0.98. If each participant independently refuses with probability $\varepsilon$, it is $(1-\varepsilon)^2$ whatever the size of the group, against $(1-\varepsilon)^n$ in the one-shot variant. The robustness has a cost of its own. Removing the refusers takes many removals, about half the group for a single refuser (Section 9.3), and although those who are removed still receive $Q$, they have lost their voice in whatever else remains to be settled.

The other side of this dilution is that refusing becomes cheap, and with that free-riding returns in a weak form. Writing $\bar{e}_h$ for the average value to $h$ of the others' ideals, holding out changes $h$'s expected value by

$$\frac{t_h + \bar{e}_h - 2\,u_h(Q)}{n}$$

compared with accepting $Q$. Holding out therefore pays exactly when the compromise is worth less to $h$ than the midpoint between $h$'s ideal and the average of the others' ideals. In the example it does not pay, since the compromise is worth 0.9 and the midpoint is 0.5: holding out is worth 0.8992, against 0.9 for accepting. If the compromise were worth only 0.4 to each participant, holding out would be worth 0.4002 in a group of a thousand, and 0.44 in a group of five. The gain shrinks with the size of the group, so the incentive is negligible in large groups and real in small ones. It also works only as long as the others accept. If every participant holds out, removals run to the end and everyone receives the lottery, which is the structure of a public goods game. The incentive is a form of the effect described in Section 7.2: when the agreement of the others serves a removed participant well, removal costs that participant little, and this makes it cheap to gamble on being the last one left.

The others have an answer to a participant for whom holding out pays. They can offer $h$ the share that $h$ holds in any case, by proposing a lottery that gives $h$'s ideal a weight of $1/n$ and $Q$ the rest. For $h$ this is worth more than holding out by $(u_h(Q) - \bar{e}_h)/n$, so $h$ prefers it whenever the compromise is worth more to them than the average of the others' ideals, which is the least one expects of a compromise. With the compromise worth 0.4, the offer is worth 0.4006 in a group of a thousand and 0.52 in a group of five, against 0.4002 and 0.44 for holding out. In the example of Section 6 the offer also costs the others less than $h$'s holding out would, leaving each of them 0.8991 instead of 0.8982. This is the structure of the consensus lotteries of Section 9.3, in which those who share a compromise pool their shares on it while the others keep their own. It answers a few holdouts and not all of them, since if every participant claims their share for their own ideal, the shares add up to the lottery again.

Hold-up and free-riding are thus two sides of one property. Where every participant is pivotal, nobody can free-ride and anybody can hold up. Where a participant who refuses can be removed, both shrink to the order of $1/n$. This is what random exclusion adds to a unanimity rule. It leaves the requirement of unanimity in place among those who are present, and it takes away the certainty of being present.

### 7.4 Two variants

The one-shot variant keeps everyone exactly at the benchmark. A stalled deliberation goes directly to the lottery, so the endowment is what each participant holds, a call is worth exactly the endowment to everyone, $L(S)$ is always acceptable, and nobody can free-ride. The price is twofold. Every agreement a smaller group could have found is lost, such as the agreement of participants 2 and 3 on $D$ in both examples. And an agreement of the whole group depends on every single participant, so that one refusal is enough to lose it.

The sequential variant recovers the agreements of smaller groups and lets an agreement survive the refusal of a few. In exchange, it lets those agreements shift value relative to the benchmark, toward removed participants when the remaining group's compromise suits them better than the lottery over its members' ideals, and away from them when it suits them worse. It also reopens a limited form of free-riding, whose gain shrinks with the size of the group.

The two variants are not on an equal footing, and the size of the group decides between them. In the one-shot variant a single call by any participant sends everyone to the lottery, so the chance of reaching an agreement falls with every member, as $(1-\varepsilon)^n$ in Section 7.3. In a large group the one-shot variant is therefore close to random ballot, and it is biased toward the lottery: one participant who prefers the lottery to the compromise, or who simply miscalculates, imposes it on everyone. The sequential variant weighs agreement and lottery more evenly. Participants who prefer the lottery can still bring it about, but only with a probability roughly in proportion to their number, about $2d/n$ when $d$ of $n$ participants refuse, and otherwise those who want the compromise obtain it.

We therefore regard the sequential variant as the mechanism proper, which is how Section 2 defines it, and the one-shot variant as a special case for small groups. Among a few people unanimity is within reach and a stray refusal is unlikely, while free-riding in the sequential variant can pay noticeably. With two participants the variants coincide. The one-shot variant also remains the reference case for the analysis, because in it the benchmark is exactly what participants hold. What the sequential variant gives up is this exactness. The value shifts of Section 7.1 need not shrink with the size of the group, and they are the cost to be weighed when the mechanism is invoked.

In the sequential variant, what participants actually hold depends on which agreements smaller groups would reach. This is an equilibrium question, and the endowment does not answer it. It supplies a benchmark that does not depend on the answer (Section 14).

### 7.5 What holds in both variants

Some guarantees survive in either variant, and they hold whatever the other participants do.

**Proposition 2 (Individual floor).** Every participant can guarantee that their own ideal is enacted with probability at least $1/n$. Consequently each participant can guarantee a value of at least

$$\frac{1}{n}\, t_i + \Big(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Big) w_i$$

where $w_i$ is the value to $i$ of their worst outcome in $\mathcal{A}$.

*Proof.* Let $i$ accept no proposal while present and keep calling for removals. Since an agreement requires $i$'s acceptance, nothing is enacted while $i$ remains. Removals are uniform, so with probability $1/n$ participant $i$ is the last one left, in which case $i$'s ideal is enacted. In the one-shot variant, $i$'s first call leads directly to the lottery, which enacts $i$'s ideal with probability $1/n$. $\square$

The bound on the value is not tight. In the one-shot variant a participant can guarantee their endowment (Theorem 4). In the sequential variant, a participant who is removed when only one other is left receives that participant's ideal and not their own worst outcome, and this happens with probability $1/n$. The exact value that $i$ can guarantee there is

$$f_i = \frac{1}{n}\, t_i + \frac{1}{n}\, \bar{e}_i + \frac{n-2}{n}\, w_i$$

where $\bar{e}_i$ is the average value to $i$ of the others' ideals, as in Section 7.3. With two participants $f_i$ equals the endowment, as it has to, since the variants then coincide.

**Proposition 3 (Coalition floor).** Any $m$ participants who share an ideal $x$ can guarantee that $x$ is enacted with probability at least $m/n$. The same holds for any $m$ participants who can rely on one another to enact $x$ if one of them is left alone.

*Proof.* The members of the coalition accept no proposal while any of them is present and keep calling. Nothing is enacted while a member remains, and with probability $m/n$ the last participant left is a member, who enacts $x$. $\square$

This is the proportionality property that Heitzig, Simmons and Constantino (2025) require of fair group decision methods. Beyond these two floors, a decision can only cover what the participants brought (Section 8), and the observation of caller independence holds in both variants.

The endowment, by contrast, is a floor only under a condition. A participant is guaranteed their endowment if, whenever they are removed, what the remaining group goes on to enact is worth at least their endowment at that point, in expectation. Given this, an induction over the size of the group together with Theorem 1 shows that the participant never holds less than their endowment. In the one-shot variant the condition holds trivially, because there are no such agreements. In the sequential variant it can fail, as the first example shows, even when nobody acts out of spite.

Two conditions that one might expect to be enough are not. The first is continuation anonymity, the condition that those who remain after a removal act only on their own preferences, without regard to who was removed. Play in the first example satisfies it, and participant 1 still falls far below their endowment. The second is a participant's own best effort. In a group of three or more, no strategy lets a participant secure their endowment whatever the others do, since the others can refuse every proposal while that participant is present, keep calling, and agree on the participant's worst outcome once they are gone. The individual floor is the correct statement of what a participant can guarantee alone.

To summarize: in either variant, each participant keeps a $1/n$ chance of their ideal, and a decision covers only what the participants brought. In the one-shot variant each participant also keeps their endowment. In the sequential variant, what a participant brought is at stake in any group that forms without them, and it may be treated better or worse there than the lottery would have treated it.

## 8. Participation and the reach of a decision

Section 7 leaves two questions. Why should anyone take part, given that what they bring can be disposed of by others? And what can a group legitimately decide, given that its decisions affect people who did not take part? Our answer to the first is that people take part because they expect to gain, and that participation is voluntary, so that nobody takes part who does not expect to. Our answer to the second is that a group's decision is valid only among its members and only over what they brought.

### 8.1 Why people take part

The main reason to join a CORE process is that there is something to gain, and two kinds of gain are involved. The first comes from coming together at all. A shared fund can pay for what no single contribution could, and a common standard is worth more to each user than any standard they could adopt alone. This gain is already contained in the endowment, since the ideals it averages over are the best things that can be done with the pool, and these are often far better than what anyone could do alone. The second gain comes from compromise. Whenever an outcome exists that everyone prefers to the lottery, deliberation can find it, and Theorem 3 shows why the lottery cannot: it gives weight only to first choices.

In the one-shot variant, the two gains separate cleanly.

**Remark (Fair share plus surplus).** In the one-shot variant, any agreement $a$ that participant $i$ accepts gives them their endowment $e_i(V)$ plus a surplus $u_i(a) - e_i(V)$. Since $i$ can obtain $e_i(V)$ by calling instead of accepting, a participant who accepts only what is worth at least that to them never receives a negative surplus.

The endowment is each participant's fair share of what the group can do, and the surplus is what agreement adds to it. The fairness results of the earlier sections matter because they make the surplus reachable: a participant who feared being exploited in the division would have no reason to look for a compromise at all. But they are not the reason to take part. The reason to take part is the expectation that, by coming together and looking for an agreement, one ends up better off than by staying apart.

That expectation holds under conditions, and they should be stated. It holds when the situation offers gains from cooperation and when no participant's ideal threatens another's stake, which Theorem 4 below makes precise. Where the conflict is purely about dividing something fixed, CORE returns the lottery (Proposition 1), and for some participants the lottery may be worse than staying out. In the sequential variant, the division of the surplus can shift toward or away from particular participants (Section 7). And compared with majority rule, a stable majority gives something up under CORE whenever there is little to gain from compromise. The case for CORE reaches such a majority only through the surplus it makes available, or through uncertainty about whether it will remain a majority (Section 11.4).

Nobody is obliged to join. A person brings something into the pool only if they expect to be better off for it, and this is what gives the floors of Section 7 their meaning: a participant knows in advance what they can guarantee themselves, and weighs that, together with what they expect beyond it, against staying out.

Voluntary participation has a consequence that should not be passed over. Someone who benefits from the way things are can decline to join. Where the others can go ahead without them, as with the standard in Section 8.3, declining blocks nothing. But where the matter can only be decided jointly, as with co-heirs who own a house together or allies bound to act by consensus, declining has the same effect as the veto that strict unanimity gives. CORE does not take that veto from people who never committed to it. It takes the veto from those who have: once the participants are bound to reach a single outcome, none of them can keep things as they are by refusing to agree. The mechanism therefore does its work where the commitment comes before the particular decision, as when a treaty, a statute, a set of bylaws or a will lays down how a class of future decisions is to be made, at a time when nobody yet knows on which side of them they will stand (Section 11.4). Where every decision needs fresh consent to the procedure itself, those who benefit from the present arrangement keep their veto, and exercise it at the stage of joining.

Let $o_i$ be the value to $i$ of staying out, keeping what they have out of the pool.

**Theorem 4 (Safe participation).** In the one-shot variant, each participant can guarantee their endowment $e_i(V)$ by accepting no proposal and calling, and cannot guarantee more. Joining is therefore safe for $i$, in the sense that it is individually rational whatever the others do, exactly when $e_i(V) \geq o_i$. In the sequential variant, what $i$ can guarantee alone is the value $f_i$ of Section 7.5, and joining is safe exactly when $f_i \geq o_i$.

*Proof.* In the one-shot variant, a call leads directly to $L(V)$, which is worth $e_i(V)$ to $i$ by Theorem 2. The others can hold $i$ to this value by refusing every proposal and calling themselves, so $e_i(V)$ is exactly what $i$ can guarantee, and comparing it with $o_i$ is what safe participation means. In the sequential variant, the others can refuse every proposal while $i$ is present, keep calling, and agree on $i$'s worst outcome as soon as $i$ is removed and at least two of them remain. Against this, the best $i$ can do is the strategy of Proposition 2. It leaves $i$ as the last participant with probability $1/n$, and as the last but one with probability $1/n$, in which case the single participant who remains enacts their own ideal. Otherwise it leaves $i$ with their worst outcome. This is worth $f_i$, and since the strategy of Proposition 2 secures at least $f_i$ against any play, $f_i$ is exactly what $i$ can guarantee. $\square$

This is the ordinary participation constraint of mechanism design, and we claim nothing new for it. What matters here is how it plays out. Suppose participant 1's ideal would impose a severe loss on participant 3 within the pool, with $u_3(a^*_1) = -30$, while $u_3(a^*_2) = 4$, $u_3(a^*_3) = 8$, and staying out is worth $3$ to participant 3. Then

$$e_3 = \frac{-30 + 4 + 8}{3} = -6 < 3$$

so participant 3's endowment is worth less than staying out. The fallback carries a one-in-three chance of their ruin, joining is not safe, and they have no reason to join. In the one-shot variant, a group whose members' ideals threaten one another's stakes simply does not form.

**Corollary 4.1 (A sufficient condition).** If $u_i(a^*_j) \geq o_i$ for every pair $i, j$, so that no participant's ideal leaves any other worse off than staying out, then joining is safe for everyone in the one-shot variant.

**Corollary 4.2 (The condition is not necessary).** A participant may find another participant's ideal ruinous and still hold an endowment above their outside option, if the remaining ideals make up for it in expectation. For example, if $u_3(a^*_1) = -6$, $u_3(a^*_2) = 4$, $u_3(a^*_3) = 8$ and $o_3 = 1$, then $u_3(a^*_1) < o_3$ while $e_3 = 2 \geq 1$. The exact condition concerns the endowment, not each ideal separately.

In the sequential variant the guaranteed floor is low, since it allows for the worst outcome in almost every case in which a participant is removed, and joining will rarely be safe in this strict sense. Participation in the sequential variant therefore rests on what people expect of one another rather than on what they can guarantee: on the expectation that the agreements of smaller groups will be compromises one can live with. This is a further reason why the choice between the variants belongs to the participants.

### 8.2 What a decision covers

A group's decision disposes of what its members brought into it and binds those who took part. It does not reach further. This is the content of the definition of $\mathcal{A}$ in Section 2: the outcomes a group can agree on are those that can be realized by acting on the pool. A group can decide how to spend a fund its members contributed. It cannot decide to spend the money of someone who did not contribute, because that money is not part of what the members can dispose of, and no procedure among them, however fair and however unanimous, can change that.

The protection of outsiders therefore does not depend on their presence, on a veto, or on their ability to leave. What is theirs was never in the pool, so there is nothing about it for the group to decide.

### 8.3 Changing what one does is not taking

It is tempting to protect outsiders more broadly, by requiring that no outcome leave any affected person worse off than they are now. We think this would be a mistake. Consider a group adopting a new technical standard, and a vendor whose business depends on the old one. The vendor is affected and may well be worse off. But nothing has been taken from them. The members of the group have only changed what they themselves do, and they were never obliged to keep using the old standard. The vendor remains free to adopt the new standard or to keep the old one. If they keep the old one and find themselves left behind, that is the ordinary consequence of living among others who make their own choices, and no procedure could prevent it without forbidding people to change what they do. A rule that protected everyone's current position would give every beneficiary of the present arrangement a veto over changing it. That is exactly the status-quo veto that makes strict unanimity a form of imposition, and the methods of Heitzig and Simmons are designed to avoid it for the same reason.

What remains open to the vendor is agreement. If they want a say in the standard, the way to have it is to take part in the decision, and helping people who affect one another reach agreement is what CORE is for. For an issue like a standard, which exists before any group forms around it, Section 10 argues that everyone, the vendor included, belongs to the population from which the participants are drawn.

### 8.4 Effects beyond the group

The line between disposing of one's own things and affecting others is rarely clean. Actions reach beyond whoever takes them. A group that pools its money to build a factory acts only on what is its own, but the smoke from the factory drifts onto the neighbors' land, and the consequences of many decisions extend to people not yet born. No group can confine the effects of its actions to itself.

What follows is that a group's decision is valid only among its members, and that it does not free them from their responsibilities toward anyone else. By agreeing to build the factory, the members settle the matter among themselves. They do not settle it with the neighbors, whose claims stand exactly as they did before. If the neighbors object, the dispute can only be resolved by a wider agreement that includes them, which means a new process in which the group is enlarged. Such agreements will often take the form of compensation, and this is the logic behind taxes on negative externalities: where an action crosses from doing one's own thing into affecting others, the crossing has to be paid for, or otherwise agreed to by those affected.

Two limits should be acknowledged. CORE does not supply a theory of where one's own sphere ends and others' begin, and enlarging a group on demand invites abuse, since someone with no real stake might claim to be affected in order to obstruct. Sections 10 and 14 take up both.

### 8.5 What a majority can do

Taken together, these points give a precise account of majority power in CORE. A majority has no instrument that a minority lacks, since calls are caller-independent. Its decisions cover only what its members brought, and they leave the claims of outsiders standing. In the one-shot variant it cannot push any participant below their endowment. In the sequential variant, a like-minded bloc can gain at the expense of participants who share no compromise with it, within the pool and never below the individual floor, while in other cases the agreements of smaller groups benefit the participants they leave out. A group choosing between the variants is also choosing how much of this redistribution to allow.

## 9. Strategy

A group whose members want different things has to bargain. No common will is waiting to be read off by the right rule. There are the different aims of the members, and there is whatever they can work out between them. Strategy, in the sense of acting with an eye to what the others will do, is therefore not a defect of particular procedures but a part of deciding together, and the theorems reviewed below confirm that no procedure able to find a compromise is free of it. What a procedure does determine is whether its own rules give some participants an advantage in that bargaining. This section sets out what the theorems establish for CORE, where they leave the strategic remainder, how the gains from agreement are divided, and why deliberation is left without rules of its own.

### 9.1 What the impossibility theorems establish

Gibbard's three theorems and Hylland's extension of the third bear on CORE in different ways, and we take them one at a time.

Gibbard (1973) shows that in every deterministic procedure with at least three possible outcomes, whatever form its ballots or moves take, either one participant dictates the outcome or some participant's best course depends on what the others do. Satterthwaite (1975) independently proved the equivalent result for voting rules over rankings. CORE is not deterministic, so the result does not apply directly, but we do not contest it.

Gibbard (1977) turns to rules that use chance, and considers those in which each voter submits a ranking of the options. He shows that every strategyproof rule of this kind is a probability mixture of unilateral rules, in which a single voter decides, and duple rules, which restrict the outcome to a fixed pair of options. If ex-post efficiency is also required, only random dictatorships remain, and among these only the uniform one treats all voters alike. For CORE this is not an obstacle but a description of its fallback. Removals that run to the end produce exactly random dictatorship, and the theorem tells us that this is essentially the only rule with those properties. CORE's benchmark is therefore the canonical choice, not an arbitrary one.

A ranking is a narrow kind of ballot, and CORE has no ballots at all. What its participants accept depends on how much they value outcomes and not only on the order in which they rank them, since weighing a compromise against a lottery is a comparison of intensities. One might hope that a procedure which gives room to intensities escapes the characterization. It does not. Gibbard (1978) extends the result from ranked ballots to game forms, that is, to any procedure that makes a lottery over the options depend on what the participants do, provided that the options and each participant's possible courses of action are finite in number. Ballots with scores are included, and so are votes taken in sequence. If such a procedure gives every participant a dominant strategy whatever their utilities, it is again, in essence, a probability mixture of unilateral and duple procedures, and if every option can be reached for certain by some combination of strategies, it is a mixture of dictatorial ones. Gibbard notes that the restriction to finitely many strategies excludes ballots on which voters write down their utilities. Hylland (1980) removes it: a rule which maps cardinal utilities to lotteries, is strategyproof, and selects an option whenever it is everyone's first choice must be a random dictatorship. Dutta, Peters and Sen (2007) give a simpler proof which, as their erratum (2008) explains, needs a mild further assumption, for instance that an option every voter ranks last is never chosen. CORE's efficient agreements meet it. Taken together, these results say that a procedure in which every participant has a dominant strategy, and in which an option that everyone ranks first is chosen, can never select a compromise. The compromises of Theorem 3 are therefore available only to procedures in which what it is best to do depends on what the others do.

CORE is a procedure of the latter kind, and it does not escape these results. Deliberation is a game in which proposing, revealing, concealing, timing and forming coalitions are all moves, and the mechanism is not strategyproof.

What can be claimed is more limited. In the one-shot variant every participant knows, without any model of anyone else's strategy, the least they need to accept. A call secures their endowment, so no proposal worth less than that has to be accepted. In the sequential variant even this floor depends on a view of what smaller groups would agree on, and what can be secured unconditionally is the individual floor of Proposition 2. Whether to accept a proposal that is worth more than the floor, or to hold out for a better one, is a strategic question in both variants, and Section 9.4 treats it as bargaining. Across the procedure, then, strategic play is possible, and in the sequential variant it includes anticipating which groups will form after which removals. Its effects are bounded by the floors of Section 7, which hold against every strategy. We call this property strategy-resilience. It is weaker than strategyproofness and different from it, and the name is meant to make that clear.

### 9.2 Incomplete information

In practice participants do not know each other's ideals or outside options, and whether to reveal, conceal or misstate them is itself part of the deliberation. One result holds cleanly in this setting.

**Proposition 4 (Bounded influence on the benchmark).** The ideal of a participant $j$ enters another participant $i$'s endowment only through the term $(1/k)\, u_i(a^*_j)$. If $j$ misrepresents their ideal, the endowment that $i$ computes from the stated ideals therefore differs from the true one by at most $(\max u_i - \min u_i)/k$, which is of order $1/n$.

*Proof.* This follows directly from the definition of the endowment. $\square$

The true endowment is not affected by what anyone states, since a participant who is left at the end enacts what they actually want. What a misstatement can move is the benchmark as the others see it, and the proposition bounds how far. It does not bound how much can be gained in bargaining, where a small shift in what each side believes it falls back on can produce a larger shift in the agreed division. What it shows is that no single participant can change what anyone takes their fair share to be by more than a proportional amount. It does not show that bargaining is fair.

Overstating one's losses carries a cost of its own. Claiming to be badly served by a proposal amounts to threatening to veto it, and in CORE a veto leads toward removals, in which the one who vetoes is as likely to lose their seat as anyone else. This makes the tactic costly, although it does not prove that it never pays.

### 9.3 Where the remainder lies

The theorems of Section 9.1 thus leave a remainder that CORE cannot eliminate, but the remainder can be located. It lies in deliberation, its effect on the benchmark is bounded, and it concentrates where participants stop talking about preferences and start insisting on constraints. A participant whose objections can be met with probability can always be accommodated, since the group can give them their share of a consensus lottery, which gives their ideal the same weight the fallback would. Heitzig, Simmons and Constantino (2025) establish an equilibrium of this kind for their MaxParC method, in which those who share a compromise pool their shares on it while the others keep their own. A participant who finds some outcome unacceptable at any probability cannot be accommodated in this way. At that point only two moves remain: someone relaxes a constraint, or the group of those holding constraints becomes smaller. The mechanism puts a precise price on the second move. Removing $d$ determined holdouts from $n$ participants takes $d(n+1)/(d+1)$ removals in expectation, about $n/2$ for a single holdout, and most of these removals fall on participants who were not holding out. Overriding a single conscientious objector thus costs the group about half its members. The first move is beyond the mechanism's reach, because constraints come to light in deliberation and only the person who holds one can give it up.

### 9.4 Dividing the gains

Even when an agreement exists, there is usually more than one. Because proposals can be lotteries, the outcomes that everyone prefers to the fallback form a whole range, and the participants have to settle on one point in it. In the example of Section 6, a participant may ask for the common second choice with a one percent chance of their own favorite instead. That raises their value from 0.9 to 0.901 and lowers everyone else's to 0.891, and both versions beat the lottery for everyone. Choosing between such agreements is a bargaining problem in the sense of Nash (1950), a game of mixed motives in the phrase of Schelling (1960): the participants share an interest in reaching some agreement and differ over which one. The gain over the fallback is collective. Its division is contested, and it is in this division that the theorems of Section 9.1 place the strategic remainder.

Bargaining theory offers several answers to how such a division should come out, and one of them follows CORE's own logic closely. Raiffa (1953) proposed a step-by-step procedure. At each step, every participant takes the best outcome available to them that leaves everyone else at their current value, and the new benchmark is the average of these outcomes, that is, the uniform lottery over what each participant would choose if they were decisive and had to respect the others' current values. This is the endowment applied again, one level up: a random dictatorship over the agreements that beat the current benchmark. The new benchmark is at least as good for everyone as the old one, since a mixture of outcomes that nobody finds worse than the benchmark is itself one, and repeating the step yields a sequence of benchmarks that rises for everyone. Diskin, Koppel and Samet (2011) show that, under standard assumptions, the sequence converges to a Pareto optimal outcome. In the example of Section 6 it converges to the common second choice.

Raiffa's procedure is not the only reasonable answer, and CORE does not prescribe it. The solutions of Nash (1950) and of Kalai and Smorodinsky (1975) are Pareto optimal and symmetric as well, and in asymmetric problems the three select different points. CORE fixes where the bargaining starts, at the endowment, but not where it ends. Applied as a binding rule to reported utilities, Raiffa's procedure would also fall under Hylland's theorem and could be manipulated. Its place is inside deliberation, as a reference point that participants may propose, which is the role Section 9.5 gives to computed compromises.

What makes Raiffa's procedure converge is that each step locks in a gain for everyone, and this is what fixed procedures lack. A ballot cast once, as in approval voting, freezes the decision at a single point. When preferences form a Condorcet cycle, which point that is depends on how voters predict one another, and predicting requires them to model each other's reasoning without end. Letting voters revise their ballots does not help by itself: with a fixed set of options that cannot be mixed, revisions can only circle the cycle, without approaching anything. Lotteries dissolve the cycle, and steps that lock in progress turn circling into convergence. CORE has both. Lotteries are admissible proposals, and since every agreement is an interim agreement (Section 2), a group can lock in what it has gained and continue from there.

Convergence of this kind takes infinitely many steps. Samet (2009) shows that bargaining by a sequence of interim agreements cannot reach its limit in finite time, in the manner of Zeno's paradox of Achilles and the tortoise. The resolution is the same as for Zeno. Every participant's sensitivity is finite, and since expected utility depends continuously on the weights of a lottery, a split of 50 percent and one of 50.000000000000001 percent are worth the same for every practical purpose. A group can stop as soon as no participant could gain more than they care about, just as a house can be placed without measuring its position to the micrometer, and each group can choose the precision it needs. Nor does a group have to walk the steps one by one. In the example of Section 6, Raiffa's procedure needs thousands of steps to come close to the common second choice, but any participant can propose that option directly, and unanimity confirms it. The procedure explains why the outcome is fair. Deliberation finds it. And there is no final destination in any case. Circumstances keep changing, and an agreement that is close enough to act on today may need adjusting tomorrow.

Chatterjee and Chaturvedi (2024) connect these ideas to the Nash solution. In their model of bargaining with interim agreements, the negotiated path of payoffs follows Raiffa's step-by-step logic, and as the frictions of bargaining vanish it converges to the Nash solution. Which point bargaining reaches depends, however, on more than the procedure. CORE gives every participant the same fallback and the same moves, which is equal bargaining power in a procedural sense. It does not equalize the other sources of bargaining power. In alternating-offer bargaining the more patient party obtains more (Rubinstein 1982), and under the Nash solution the less risk-averse party does (Kihlstrom, Roth and Schmeidler 1981). The same holds inside CORE. The worse the lottery is for a participant compared with the compromises on offer, the more that participant loses if agreement fails, and the more they may concede to secure it. These asymmetries are not specific to CORE, since every bargaining process has them, but they mean that equal procedural power is what CORE guarantees, and not more.

### 9.5 Why deliberation is left undefined

The mechanism specifies two things precisely: what counts as an agreement, and what happens when someone calls for a removal. It specifies nothing else. In particular it does not say how deliberation proceeds, who may propose what, in what order proposals are taken up, or how participants come to recognize that they agree. This is a deliberate choice, and there are two reasons for it.

The first reason comes from the theorems of Section 9.1. Some strategic remainder exists in any procedure of this kind, and it cannot be removed. It can, however, be placed. Every formal rule added to a procedure is a place where the remainder can settle, and once it is built into a rule it is fixed and known in advance, so that whoever understands the rule best, or controls how it is applied, can use it. Taking proposals in a fixed order is the clearest example. If each proposal is put to a vote and rejected until one passes, the order decides which of the acceptable proposals wins, and whoever sets the order holds an advantage that the rules themselves create. Under majority rule the effect can be extreme. McKelvey (1976) shows that, under standard assumptions about preferences, when options vary in more than one dimension and no option beats every other by a majority, an agenda setter can lead a sequence of majority votes from any starting point to any outcome. Under unanimity the effect is weaker but does not disappear, since the order still decides which of several acceptable proposals the group reaches first.

The pattern is general. Even a majority vote between two options, in which no voter can gain by voting against their preference, presupposes that someone has chosen the two options. Under plurality voting, similar candidates divide their common support between them (Tideman 1987), so that who is nominated can decide who wins, and a group that expects to prevail has reason to shape the nominations before any vote is cast. In neither case is the difficulty that people bargain. They have to, since they want different things, and no rule can read off from their ballots a common will that is not there (Riker 1982). The difficulty is that a rule which computes the outcome stands above the bargaining. Whatever the participants work out among themselves beforehand, the rule has the last word, and it is rarely neutral among them. Whoever is favored by the rule, or is in a position to steer it, bargains from a stronger position and holds a share of the power the rule has over everyone.

CORE therefore specifies only the two parts whose effects can be made clean. In the one-shot variant, the least a participant needs to accept is fixed by their endowment, and in both variants a call has the same effect whoever makes it. Everything else is left open, so that the remainder those theorems guarantee is not fixed in any rule. It lies in deliberation, where the rules privilege no move and each move can be answered by another: a counter-proposal, the disclosure of something concealed, or a call. This does not make deliberation fair, and the preceding sections say what is bounded and what is not. It ensures that no advantage is written into the procedure before anyone has spoken.

The result is a procedure whose rules select an outcome on their own in one case only, the fallback, and there strategy has no hold: the draw cannot be influenced, and whoever is left enacts what they want most. Every other outcome is selected by the participants themselves, unanimously. CORE contains no majority rule and no supermajority rule, and it contains no minority rule either, since among participants committed to a single outcome, blocking an agreement does not keep things as they are. No participant and no group can force an outcome. All that anyone can force is the step from deliberation to the draw, that is, from bargaining, which is strategic, to chance, which is not. What a group can gain from the draw itself is limited to its proportional share in the one-shot variant, and to the shifts described in Sections 7.1 and 8.5 in the sequential one.

The second reason comes from Theorem 3. The gains from agreement can come from compromises that the lottery never selects, and these need not have been on any list when deliberation began. Any rule that fixed the options in advance, or the order in which they are considered, would confine the search to what someone had already thought of and could leave out the very outcomes from which the gains come. No rule can deliver the result of the search ahead of time, since the result may be an option nobody has yet conceived of. The search can be cut off, but it cannot be cut short. The call is what cuts it off. Any participant can end the search in the current group at any moment, and by Theorem 1 doing so moves nobody's benchmark. How long deliberation lasts is thereby left to the participants rather than set by a deadline, which would be a formal rule of its own, favoring whoever benefits from the default when time runs out.

None of this prevents participants from using structured methods within deliberation. A facilitator, a straw poll, a mediator's proposal or a computed compromise, such as the outcome of Raiffa's procedure in Section 9.4, can all help the search along. What matters is that none of them binds. Their results are inputs to deliberation, and the only acts with formal consequences are unanimous acceptance and the call.

The design reflects an intuition familiar from the incompleteness results of logic: a formal system rich enough to capture what matters leaves something outside its own reach, and the question is not whether such a remainder exists but where it falls. We do not claim that CORE is an instance of Gödel's theorems. The formal results that apply are Gibbard's and Hylland's, and whether the resemblance is more than an analogy is a question we leave open. The design follows the same pattern nevertheless. The formal rules are built around a part that is left undefined, so that what they cannot eliminate falls where it can be seen and answered rather than where it can be exploited.

## 10. Scope

Everything so far assumed a given set of participants and a given pool. We now ask how these are determined. The question cannot be postponed, because Section 8 made the validity of a decision depend on who took part in it.

It helps to distinguish two situations, according to whether the object of a decision exists before the group or is created by it.

In the first situation, a group comes together and in doing so creates the thing it decides about. A fund exists because people paid into it, and a decision about how to use it concerns exactly those who paid in. There is no question of who should take part, because forming the group settled it.

In the second, the issue exists before any group forms around it. Which internet standard to adopt concerns everyone who will use the network, and no group can declare itself to be the set of those affected. Any boundary drawn here would require a substantive judgment about who counts, and that is precisely the kind of judgment the framework has no authority to make.

For issues of the second kind, the default has to be the only set of participants that needs no justification, which is everyone. In practice this means sortition: a uniformly random sample of the whole population deliberates under CORE on everyone's behalf. Section 13.1 shows that drawing such a sample is the same operation as carrying out CORE's removals in advance, so that the sample inherits the mechanism's fairness properties, together with the caveats of Section 7.

Uniformity appears here for the same reason it appears throughout the mechanism. The benchmark is uniform over the participants' ideals, each removal is a uniform draw over participants, the sample is uniform over persons, and the default population is everyone. A uniform distribution is the one choice that needs no justification, and every departure from it has to be earned by a decision that is itself legitimate.

This makes it possible to narrow the set of participants without circularity. An assembly may hand a decision to a smaller body, and the handover is legitimate because the assembly that made it was legitimately constituted. The regress of asking who decides who decides ends with the population as a whole, which needs no prior decision to constitute it. One starts from everyone and narrows only by steps that are themselves justified.

A universal assembly is far too costly to convene for most questions, and in practice it will almost never meet. It does not need to. A standing arrangement that determines who takes part in which decisions is legitimate to the extent that a universal assembly would endorse it, and open to challenge to the extent that it would not. The universal assembly thus plays the same role as the fallback in Section 5: it is rarely used, and it does its work by being available. This is what makes it coherent to establish such arrangements over time and to renegotiate them when needed. The parallel is one of structure rather than a shared theorem, since one concerns feasibility and the other legitimacy.

Three further points refine this picture.

First, handing a decision to a smaller body should be understood as delegation rather than transfer. Narrowing can mean two different things. It can be a finding of fact, that only these people are affected, or a delegation of trust, that everyone is affected but these people are better placed to decide. The internet standard is of the second kind: the standard binds every user, so the engineers who decide it act as trustees for all of them. The distinction matters for the risk of capture. If narrowing were a finding of fact, a captured assembly could give its authority away permanently. A delegation, by contrast, can be revoked by the same procedure that granted it, and this is what keeps trusteeship from turning into a transfer of power. Narrowing should therefore be presumed to be delegation.

Second, a randomly drawn assembly can be trusted as a trustee, but only to a degree, and Sections 7 and 8 say to what degree. A random sample represents the population in distribution, yet any particular sample may be partial in ways its members neither notice nor control. This objection weighs heavily against a sample that decides by majority over an unrestricted range of outcomes. It weighs less against a sample that decides by CORE, because the sample's decisions cover only what was placed in its hands and leave everyone else's claims standing, and because every member of the population keeps the same floors as in direct participation (Section 13.1). What sortition cannot guarantee is that a particular sample's agreement serves those outside it as well as the lottery would. Those outside the sample are in the position of removed participants in Section 7, and the sample's agreement may be better or worse for them than their endowment. Jacobs and Heitzig (2024) note that the legitimacy of outcomes chosen partly by chance is the least developed part of the case for non-deterministic methods. Our answer is that chance decides who deliberates and resolves what deliberation cannot, while legitimacy comes from the uniformity of the draw and from the limits on what any draw can decide.

Third, the two situations distinguished above describe how the boundary of a decision is drawn, not two kinds of decision. A group that forms around something it creates can still produce effects beyond it, as the factory in Section 8.4 does. The first situation holds only as long as a decision's effects stay within what the group brought. Once they reach others, those others acquire a claim, and settling it requires a wider process that includes them. When a group is enlarged in this way, the enlargement should be symmetric: it should take in everyone the widening reaches, rather than a selection of them, so that nobody can shape the outcome by choosing whom to include.

Finally, an assembly must be free to change or ignore the question it has been given. Setting the agenda is the most powerful way of narrowing a decision without justification, and it survives even when the participants are drawn correctly, since a body that can only answer the question put to it has had its options limited by whoever posed it. The same principle applies as elsewhere: by default an assembly may take up any question, and any restriction needs a justification.

## 11. Interpretation: a veil of uncertainty

This section offers an interpretation of the mechanism rather than further results. It connects the endowment to a familiar idea in political philosophy, and it says where the connection holds and where it does not.

### 11.1 A veil over decisiveness

Before any agreement is reached, a participant does not know whether they will be the one whose ideal is enacted, or on which side of each removal they will land. The endowment is the value of facing this uncertainty. In that sense the mechanism places its participants behind a veil, and the veil is real rather than hypothetical: the uncertainty comes from an actual draw, not from a thought experiment. It also forms anew at every stage. After each removal, those who remain face the same uncertainty again, in a smaller group. The sortition draw of Section 13.1 is the same veil, drawn over the question of who deliberates at all.

What the veil covers is narrow. Participants know who they are, what they want, and usually what the others want. The only thing hidden from them is who will be decisive if agreement fails. This distinguishes the mechanism from the best-known veils in the literature. Rawls (1971) hides from the parties who they are, and argues that behind his veil they would reason by maximin, choosing principles that make the worst position as good as possible. Harsanyi (1953, 1955) gives an impartial observer an equal chance of being anyone and has them maximize expected utility, which leads to average utilitarianism. CORE shares Harsanyi's equal probabilities and expected utility, in the form of the endowment, but neither conclusion. A proposal is accepted only if every participant prefers it to their own fallback, which is a Pareto condition relative to the lottery, and neither maximin nor maximization of the average. For this reason we do not call the mechanism Rawlsian, natural as the image of the veil makes that label.

The closest relative is the veil of uncertainty of constitutional economics. Buchanan and Tullock (1962) argue that people choosing the rules under which they will later make decisions do not know what their positions under those rules will be, and that this real uncertainty moves them toward rules that are fair to every position. CORE's uncertainty is of the same kind, real and about position, but it operates within each decision and not only at the stage of choosing rules. Müller (1998) questions whether such a veil is needed, arguing that players who know their interests fully can still reach Pareto-efficient agreements, and that the veil can create problems of its own. The mechanism has a partial answer. Its veil does not produce agreement by keeping participants ignorant of their interests. Agreement comes from the gap between the lottery and a compromise, and the only job of the veil is to make the fallback against which that gap is measured a fair one.

### 11.2 The prisoner's dilemma

The prisoner's dilemma shows the veil at work. Two players each choose whether to cooperate or defect. Write $T$ for the temptation payoff of defecting against a cooperator, $R$ for the reward of mutual cooperation, $P$ for the punishment of mutual defection, and $S$ for the payoff of cooperating against a defector, with $T > R > P > S$. These letters are used only in this section. Suppose both players bring their conduct into a CORE process, so that the outcome is a pair of actions they are committed to carrying out.

If they cannot agree, one of them is removed and the other enacts their ideal, which is to defect while the other cooperates. The fallback is therefore not mutual defection but a coin toss over who exploits whom, worth $(T + S)/2$ to each player. Mutual cooperation is worth $R$, and it beats the fallback for both players exactly when

$$2R > T + S$$

This is the condition usually added to the definition of the prisoner's dilemma, for instance by Axelrod (1984), to ensure that mutual cooperation is better than taking turns exploiting each other. Under CORE the same condition becomes the condition for agreement. Neither player knows in advance on which side of the defection they would land, and both prefer cooperating to finding out.

The example also shows what the mechanism does not do. It does not solve the problem of enforcement. The players must already be committed to carrying out whatever is decided, and what CORE changes is only what they fall back on when they cannot agree. Given such a commitment, mutual cooperation can also be reached by other procedures, such as bargaining with mutual defection as the fallback. What CORE adds is a fallback defined in the same way for every decision, which in games less symmetric than this one treats the players alike where mutual defection need not.

### 11.3 The veil in the two variants

The two variants of Section 7 correspond to two ways in which the veil can lift. In the one-shot variant it stays closed until it lifts completely: a stalled deliberation goes straight to the lottery, and nobody learns anything about their position before the outcome is drawn. In the sequential variant it lifts a little with each removal. Those who remain after a removal know that they remain, and they can look for an agreement among themselves with that knowledge. This is exactly why their agreements can shift value toward or away from the participant who was removed. It is also why free-riding can return in the sequential variant (Section 7.3). A participant who expects those who remain to agree on something that suits them loses little by being removed, and can therefore afford to gamble on being the last one left. And it is why a single refusal no longer decides everything, since those who remain can still agree once the refuser is gone. The choice between the variants is a choice between keeping the veil closed and letting smaller groups act on what they learn as it opens.

### 11.4 The veil and the choice of rule

The veil also appears one level up, in the choice of whether to use CORE at all. Section 13.3 notes that majorities have little reason to give up majority rule. But a group that decides many questions over time rarely knows in advance which side of each question its members will be on. A member of today's majority may belong to tomorrow's minority, and someone uncertain of this has reason to prefer a rule under which no majority can impose on a minority. This is Buchanan and Tullock's argument for choosing rules under uncertainty, applied to CORE. It does not reach a permanent majority, one that knows it will prevail on every question that matters to it, and for such a majority there is no formal reason to adopt the mechanism.

### 11.5 Safe and worthwhile

The veil and the positive-sum argument of Section 8.1 fit together. The veil makes the fallback fair, and a fair fallback makes cooperation safe, since nobody has to fear that the others will use the threat of disagreement against them. The gains from coming together and from compromise make cooperation worthwhile. Neither is enough alone. A fair fallback with nothing to gain above it gives nobody a reason to come, and a large potential gain without a fair fallback invites everyone to fight over its division.

## 12. Relation to existing work

The closest existing work is the research program of Heitzig and Simmons on non-deterministic consensus methods, and CORE is best read against it.

Heitzig and Simmons (2012) introduce a method they call "Full consensus or Random Ballot fall-back". Each voter submits a consensus ballot and a fall-back ballot. If some option is marked on every consensus ballot, it wins; otherwise a randomly drawn fall-back ballot decides. They take the uniform lottery over the voters' favorites as their benchmark, define potential consensus options as the options everyone prefers to it, and prove that, under a strong coalitional equilibrium concept, the equilibria elect such options whenever one exists. This is the one-shot variant of CORE with a fixed set of options, and their benchmark is the endowment of Section 3. Theorem 2, and the observation that the fallback does its work counterfactually, are in substance theirs.

Heitzig, Simmons and Constantino (2025) extend the program with two further methods. The Nash Lottery bargains automatically over lotteries, and MaxParC uses conditional commitments to approve compromise options. Both satisfy a proportionality guarantee: any $m$ of $N$ voters can secure a chance of at least $m/N$ for an option they support, which is the coalition floor of Section 7.5. The authors report simulations indicating that the welfare cost of this fairness is small, and a laboratory experiment in which a sizable fraction of participants preferred a non-deterministic method to plurality voting, while participants who were in the majority strongly preferred majoritarian methods.

Relative to this program, CORE contributes in three ways. The first is sequential exclusion. Their methods are one-shot, whereas CORE lets smaller groups agree on the way to the fallback. Theorem 1, the observation of caller independence, Corollary 1.3, the cost of removing holdouts in Section 9.3, and the analysis of Section 7, including the robustness of agreement to refusal, are specific to that structure. The second is open deliberation. Their methods work with a fixed set of options and ballots, while CORE's participants propose freely, and part of the value of the mechanism lies in options that were on no list at the outset. This makes CORE harder to analyze, and it is why we cite the equilibrium results of Heitzig and Simmons rather than reproving them for our setting. Section 9.5 explains why we leave deliberation open nonetheless. The third is scope. Section 8's account of what a decision covers and Section 10's account of who takes part address questions their methods take as given.

Gibbard (1977) characterizes random dictatorship as essentially the only strategyproof and ex-post efficient randomized rule over rankings. Gibbard (1978) extends the characterization to procedures of any form with finitely many strategies, and Hylland (1980) and Dutta, Peters and Sen (2007) extend it to rules that use cardinal utilities and satisfy only unanimity, which is why the benchmark is canonical rather than chosen. Pereira's (2023) COWPEA Lottery narrows the set of candidates by random ballots much as CORE narrows the set of participants, but because it works with fixed approval ballots it leaves compromises open to the free-rider problem of Section 6. Section 9.5 draws on Tideman's (1987) analysis of vote splitting and on Riker's (1982) argument that the outcome of a vote cannot be read as the will of the people. Section 9.4 draws on the bargaining literature, in particular on Raiffa's (1953) step-by-step solution and its analysis by Diskin, Koppel and Samet (2011), on Samet's (2009) argument about interim agreements, and on Chatterjee and Chaturvedi (2024). Jacobs and Heitzig (2024) set out the philosophical case for non-deterministic elections, emphasizing proportional power and stronger reasons to vote, and note that the legitimacy of outcomes chosen partly by chance has received little attention. Section 10 is addressed in part to that gap.

## 13. Outlook

### 13.1 Sortition

Section 10 proposed that issues without a natural group of participants should be decided by a random sample of the whole population. Sortition is not a separate mechanism added to CORE, however. It is CORE's own removal step, carried out before deliberation begins. Removing $n - k$ participants from $V$ one at a time, each drawn uniformly from those present, leaves a set of $k$ participants that is uniformly distributed over all subsets of that size, and drawing a sortition sample of size $k$ produces exactly the same distribution. The only difference is timing. In CORE, removals happen when deliberation stalls. In sortition, they happen in advance, to bring the group down to a size at which deliberation is practical.

Several results therefore carry over directly.

**Proposition 5 (Sortition preserves the benchmark).** Let $S$ be a uniformly random subset of $V$ of size $k$. Then the expected value of $e_i(S)$ equals $e_i(V)$ for every $i$ in $V$.

*Proof.* Each $j$ in $V$ belongs to $S$ with probability $k/n$, so the expected value of $e_i(S)$ is $(1/k) \sum_{j \in V} (k/n)\, u_i(a^*_j) = e_i(V)$. The same result follows by applying Theorem 1 $n - k$ times. $\square$

The floors of Section 7.5 hold as well. A person is drawn into the sample with probability $k/n$ and, once drawn, can secure a $1/k$ chance of being the last participant left, which gives $1/n$ overall. The same calculation, averaged over how many members of a coalition are drawn, gives $m/n$ for a coalition of $m$.

The caveat of Section 7 carries over too, and in practice it may be the most important consequence of that section. Everyone who is not drawn stands where a removed participant stands: they receive whatever the sample agrees on. Averaged over the draw, their benchmark is exactly what it would be if everyone deliberated together. But the agreement of a particular sample can be worse for them than their endowment, if the sample's compromise is one they dislike, or better, if it suits them more than a lottery over the sample members' ideals would. The first example of Section 7 can be read in exactly this way, as a sample of two drawn from three. Sortition therefore gives each person fairness in expectation over the draw, the floors, and the limits of Section 8 on what any sample can decide. It does not guarantee that a particular sample will serve them as well as the lottery.

### 13.2 Delegation

Sortition makes the selection of participants proportional, but the person who is drawn may not want, or be able, to serve, or may know someone who would represent their concerns better than they could. Delegation addresses this, under three rules.

The first rule is that whoever holds a seat serves in it, unless they pass it on to someone of their choosing, who then holds it on the same terms. A seat can change hands at any time, before or during deliberation, and at every moment it has exactly one holder. Nothing waits for a chain of passes to come to an end: at each moment someone holds the seat and carries the responsibility of serving in it. A seat cannot be abandoned, only passed on.

The second rule is that nobody holds more than one seat, so a seat can only be passed to someone who does not already hold one. This is what separates the proposal from liquid democracy, in which votes accumulate along chains of delegation and a few delegates can come to carry a large share of the total weight. Kahng, Mackenzie and Procaccia (2021) show that such concentration can make delegation perform worse than direct voting, even when everyone delegates only to someone better informed than themselves. With one seat per person, passing a seat changes who holds it but never how much weight anyone has, and concentration of this kind cannot arise.

The third rule is that a person who has passed a seat on cannot take another seat, although they can take back the one they passed, as described below. Passing a seat is a way of stepping out of serving, and it should not be possible to return by a different route. The rule matters all the more because passes can be revoked: someone who could take back one seat while holding another would in effect have two voices. It also rules out cycles. A seat can never be passed to anyone who has passed a seat before, so every chain of passes is a finite sequence of distinct people.

Together, these rules mean that each person belongs to at most one chain, and that each chain acts as a group represented by whoever currently holds its seat. A person who passes their seat on declares that, in a deliberation between the two of them, they would accept their successor's proposal, and a chain is a nested sequence of such declarations. The seat carries the current holder's ideal into the benchmark and their acceptance into every decision, and through them the whole chain is represented. If the seat is drawn for removal, the chain loses its voice together.

Revocation respects this nesting. Anyone in a chain can take the seat back. It then returns to them, and the part of the chain that followed them ends: those who received the seat through them no longer represent it. The person originally drawn can therefore always reclaim the seat, and each later member of the chain has the same power over everyone after them. This parallels Section 10, where an assembly can take back a question it has handed to a smaller body. We adopt revocability provisionally, because how it behaves in use needs closer analysis (Section 14).

Passing a seat also fits the rest of the mechanism. It is the voluntary counterpart of a removal. A removal takes a random seat out of the body, while a pass keeps the seat and replaces its holder with someone the holder has chosen. Since removals are draws over seats, passing a seat does not help it escape removal: whoever holds a seat when it is drawn leaves with it. And because a seat carries a single ideal, a pass or a revocation can shift anyone else's endowment by no more than the bound of Proposition 4, the range of their utilities divided by the number of seats.

Delegation also offers a way out when deliberation is stuck. A holder who does not know what to decide can pass their seat to someone who might. A body that recognizes that none of its members can find a solution can step aside together, and the process can begin again with a new sample.

We do not develop delegation further here, for three reasons. First, although passing seats cannot concentrate weight, it does change the composition of the body. People are likely to pass their seats to those they trust, and over repeated passes seats may drift toward a particular kind of person, such as the well connected, the most committed or, as one may hope, the most capable and wise. Nothing we have shown guarantees that the views represented in the body stay distributed as they were after the draw. Through the composition of the body, delegation also changes who is compatible with whom after a removal, and Section 7 shows that this can shift value between participants. Second, a holder brings their own judgment, their own alliances and their own proposals. Nested revocation keeps every link of a chain accountable to those before it, which turns the principal-agent problem into the familiar question of whether principals notice when they are poorly served and act on it. It softens the problem without removing it. Third, a seat that can be passed on can also be sold, and nothing in the mechanism prevents this. One seat per person limits what a buyer can hold themselves, but not how many seats they can steer toward people who will do their bidding. Revocation makes a sale harder to enforce, since anyone earlier in the chain could take the seat back, but a buyer could guard against that by paying only for as long as the seat stays passed, so it is no answer on its own. Until seat-buying has an answer, delegation cannot be recommended without reservation.

### 13.3 Adoption

The experiment of Heitzig, Simmons and Constantino points to a problem that formal analysis cannot reach. Invoking CORE is a commitment, and those best placed to refuse it are the people who benefit from majority rule. A mechanism that protects minorities has to be adopted by groups that include majorities. The universal default of Section 10 helps where an issue exists before any group, since no majority then holds the right to set the procedure. Where a group is already organized under majority rule, the path to adopting CORE runs through that majority's consent. Section 11.4 gives one reason a majority might consent, namely uncertainty about whether it will remain a majority, but for a majority that is sure of prevailing there is no formal reason to expect it.

The same holds for anyone who benefits from the present arrangement, whether or not they belong to a majority. Since joining is voluntary, they can decline whenever a particular decision threatens what they have, and CORE binds them only if the commitment was made before that decision came up (Section 8.1). Adoption therefore has to happen at the level of rules: in the founding document of an association, in a treaty, or in a statute that governs a class of decisions. These are the settings in which the uncertainty of Section 11.4 is greatest, and in which a rule that favors no position has the best chance of being accepted by all.

## 14. Open problems

**What participants actually hold in the sequential variant.** The endowment is a benchmark, and in the sequential variant what participants actually hold depends on which agreements smaller groups would reach. Characterizing that equilibrium, or bounding it more tightly than the individual floor does, is the main technical question we leave open. A recursive definition that works upward from the smallest groups would need a rule for which agreement each group selects among those available to it, and the mechanism supplies no such rule.

**The voiceless and the absent.** Future generations, the dead, non-human beings, and people who do not know that a decision is being made cannot be sampled and cannot join. Section 8.4 leaves their claims standing, but a claim needs someone to press it, and these parties cannot object or take part in a wider agreement. A participant may bind themselves to advocate for an absent party, carrying that party's claim together with a share in its consequences, which makes advocacy honest rather than costless. It does not make advocacy happen. The mechanism can give representation of the absent a proper form, but it cannot supply the representatives.

**Where one's own sphere ends.** Section 8 depends on a distinction between disposing of one's own things and affecting others, and people frequently disagree about where it lies. Whether the neighbors' claim includes clean air, or whether a settled community's use of land is an entitlement or an expectation, are questions on which the most serious conflicts turn, and the mechanism inherits them rather than resolving them. Framing the question in terms of what is someone's own, rather than what someone is used to, makes it the right question. It does not answer it.

**The population boundary.** Sampling requires a population, and "everyone the decision could affect" does the same work that "affected" did before. The change is still an improvement, since a coarse boundary on the population is more robust than deciding standing case by case, and a bad actor diluted in a large sample is far less dangerous than one holding an individual veto. But the boundary still has to be drawn, and the enlargements of Section 8.4 raise the same question for groups that grow in response to claims. The constraint that seems right is symmetry: a widening should take in everyone it reaches rather than a selection of them, so that the scope of a decision cannot be tailored to a preferred result. If one neighbor is included, so is the neighbor on the other side. This is a formal constraint rather than a substantive judgment about who counts, which is why the framework can adopt it, but it presupposes a notion of who is near whom that will differ from domain to domain.

**Revoking a passed seat.** Section 13.2 makes passes revocable along the chain, and we adopt this provisionally. The rules are consistent in a basic sense: every seat always has exactly one holder, and every chain is a finite sequence of distinct people. But we have not analyzed how they behave in use. The possibility of being replaced may make holders more responsive to those before them in the chain, or merely more cautious. Revocations and new passes during deliberation might become frequent enough to disrupt it. And influence within a chain is ordered, since those earlier in the chain can always overrule those later in it. That is intended, but we have not studied what it does to the body as a whole.

**Framing the question.** The principle that an assembly may take up any question gives the right shape, but we have not specified a procedure by which a body revises its own agenda without that revision becoming a new act of agenda-setting.

## 15. Summary

The endowment is the fair benchmark: the value of the lottery over the participants' ideals that removals would lead to if agreement never came, in which each of $k$ participants has a $1/k$ chance of being the one who decides. A call for removal leaves every endowment unchanged, and it is the same random event whoever makes it, so no participant holds an instrument that others lack. Unanimous agreement exchanges the lottery for an outcome everyone prefers to it. The lottery counts only first choices, so the gains from agreement come from outcomes that deserve more weight than their share of first choices, among them compromises the lottery never selects. This makes deliberation the substance of the mechanism, and it limits the mechanism's advantage to cases where compromise exists at all.

People take part because coming together and finding a compromise can leave everyone better off than staying apart, and the guarantees are the floor beneath that expectation. Read as a veil of uncertainty over who will be decisive, the mechanism makes the fallback fair, and a fair fallback is what makes the search for that gain safe.

In the one-shot variant, where a stalled deliberation goes straight to the lottery, the benchmark is exactly what participants hold, but an agreement depends on every single participant, and one refusal is enough to lose it. In the sequential variant, smaller groups can find agreements the lottery would miss, and an agreement survives the refusal of a few. In exchange, those agreements can shift value toward or away from the participants they leave out, and a weak form of free-riding returns, whose gain shrinks with the size of the group. In both variants, each participant keeps a $1/n$ chance of their ideal, and any $m$ participants who share an option keep $m/n$ for it. In a large group a single refusal is almost certain, so the one-shot variant comes close to random ballot there, and the sequential variant is the one we recommend for all but small groups.

A decision covers only what the participants brought into it and binds only those who took part. It leaves the claims of everyone else standing, and those claims are settled by wider agreements rather than overridden. No one's current position is protected as such, so no one gains a veto merely because the present arrangement suits them. Participation is voluntary, and in the one-shot variant a group whose members' ideals threaten one another's stakes does not form. For the same reason, the mechanism removes the veto of the status quo only among those who committed to it before the decision at hand.

Above the mechanism, the same principle of uniformity determines who deliberates. Groups that create what they decide about draw their own boundary, issues that exist before any group default to everyone, and every narrowing has to be a justified decision rather than an assumption. Sortition, the practical form of that default, is CORE's own removal step carried out in advance.

CORE does not escape the impossibility theorems of Gibbard and Hylland, and no procedure that finds compromises could. It is not an algorithm that computes a result from ballots but a frame for bargaining, which gives everyone the same fallback and the same moves. Its rules select an outcome on their own only in the fallback, where strategy has no hold. No participant and no group can force an outcome, and all that anyone can force is the step from deliberation to the draw. The mechanism locates the strategic remainder in the division of the gains from agreement, bounds each participant's influence on the benchmark by their proportional share, and leaves the rest to deliberation, where it can be seen and answered. Deliberation is left without formal rules for this reason, and because the compromises from which gains come cannot be listed in advance. The search can be cut off by a call, but it cannot be cut short. It need not run to its limit either. Since everyone's sensitivity is finite, an agreement close enough to act on is enough, and like every agreement under CORE it is an interim one.
